## Geopolitics, Oil, Inflation, and the Fed

The 60-day U.S.–Iran Memorandum of Understanding expired on Monday, 17 August 2026, without a permanent peace deal or a restored, open Strait of Hormuz. The Associated Press reported that the deal President Donald Trump signed at Versailles in June had set a 60-day deadline for ending the war and reaching a nuclear accord, that the deadline was Monday, and that “the two sides are further apart than they were then,” with talks focused on reopening Hormuz and lifting a U.S. blockade that had been supposed to happen under the interim deal ([AP News](https://apnews.com/article/iran-us-war-diplomacy-deal-deadline-nuclear-258e9c556fc861c05ccf6ed2b38515d6) (accessed 2026-08-20, **Primary**/Established, confidence: High)). Reuters, reprinted by Al-Monitor, dated the MoU to 17 June and said that period “elapsed on Monday and Trump said he had no plans to extend it” ([Al-Monitor / Reuters](https://www.al-monitor.com/originals/2026/08/trump-says-no-talks-planned-iran-tehran-says-strait-hormuz-still-shut) (accessed 2026-08-20, Established, confidence: High)). Al Jazeera likewise dated expiry to Monday amid faltering talks ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/17/us-iran-memorandum-of-understanding-expires-how-and-why-it-fell-apart) (accessed 2026-08-20, Established, confidence: High)). On Tuesday, 18 August, Trump posted on Truth Social: “There are no talks or conversations going on, or scheduled, with the Islamic Republic of Iran. The Naval Blockade remains in full force and effect. The Hormuz Strait is open and operating. All water mines have been removed or detonated” ([Trump’s Truth archive of Truth Social](https://trumpstruth.org/statuses/40962) (accessed 2026-08-20, **Primary** for the post text, confidence: High); same wording in [Al-Monitor / Reuters](https://www.al-monitor.com/originals/2026/08/trump-says-no-talks-planned-iran-tehran-says-strait-hormuz-still-shut)). Iranian officials contradicted the “open” claim: Mohammad Baqer Qalibaf said the strait would remain shut until the United States met June-deal conditions, including ending the port blockade and oil sanctions ([Al-Monitor / Reuters](https://www.al-monitor.com/originals/2026/08/trump-says-no-talks-planned-iran-tehran-says-strait-hormuz-still-shut)). AP independently reported that traffic through Hormuz, after rising immediately after the interim deal, was “back to a small fraction of what it was before the war” and that fuel and other goods prices were expected to climb further the longer that continued ([AP News](https://apnews.com/article/iran-us-war-diplomacy-deal-deadline-nuclear-258e9c556fc861c05ccf6ed2b38515d6)).

Kpler ship-tracking is the source of the “handful of ships versus ~130 prewar” comparison, but daily counts on the expiry weekend are not identical across outlets. The National, citing “the latest Kpler data,” reported only three vessels transiting on Sunday (16 August), down from five on Saturday and 12 on Friday, versus a post-MoU weekly peak of 275 crossings in the week of 24 June and a collapse into single-digit daily crossings by mid-August; it also quoted Kpler trade-risk analyst Ana Subasic on a six-to-eight-week backlog of about 520 commercial vessels if unrestricted traffic resumes ([The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/energy/2026/08/17/hormuz-traffic-falls-to-single-digits-as-60-day-deadline-for-us-iran-mou-expires/) (accessed 2026-08-20, Established, Kpler as **Primary** data vendor via this report, confidence: Medium on the Sunday “three” print)). Reuters, in a 16–17 August Hormuz shipping story summarized in search results and echoed by secondary reprints, reported five commodity vessels on Saturday and none registered for Sunday, “versus 31 in the prior weekend,” and said those figures were “far from the more than 130 ships a day that traversed the Strait of Hormuz before the war launched by the U.S. and Israel on Iran in February” ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/shipping-via-hormuz-strait-slows-after-tanker-attacks-data-shows-2026-08-16/) (accessed 2026-08-20 via search snippet; full page returned 401, confidence: Medium)). Al-Monitor’s Reuters copy on 18 August added that “preliminary shipping data on Tuesday showed ship crossings via the Strait of Hormuz were still in single digits on Monday” ([Al-Monitor / Reuters](https://www.al-monitor.com/originals/2026/08/trump-says-no-talks-planned-iran-tehran-says-strait-hormuz-still-shut)). Direct kpler.com dashboards were not retrieved. The operational conclusion that is common to these sources is that tracked Hormuz traffic in mid-August was a small fraction of the prewar ~130-ship daily pace, not that any single Sunday print is settled.

Oil prices in the 1–20 August window stayed elevated relative to early-2026 levels and firmed into the MoU expiry. EIA daily spots through 18 August show WTI Cushing at $86.04 on 17 August and $86.48 on 18 August, and Brent Europe at $92.43 and $95.29 on those dates ([EIA PET_PRI_SPT](https://www.eia.gov/dnav/pet/PET_PRI_SPT_S1_D.htm) (accessed 2026-08-20, **Primary**, confidence: High for those dates)). EIA’s “Today in Energy” wholesale spots for the 19 August close listed WTI at $87.28 (+0.9%) and Brent at $92.37 (−3.1%) ([EIA Today in Energy prices](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/prices.php) (accessed 2026-08-20 via search snippet, **Primary**, confidence: Medium pending a successful full-page fetch)). Futures prints on 18–20 August sit in a nearby but not identical band: Reuters via Al-Monitor said Brent futures settled “just over $91” on Tuesday, 18 August, after a wartime peak of $126 ([Al-Monitor / Reuters](https://www.al-monitor.com/originals/2026/08/trump-says-no-talks-planned-iran-tehran-says-strait-hormuz-still-shut)); Reuters on 20 August had Brent futures up 1.54% to $93.06 as Hormuz disruption showed “few signs of easing” ([Reuters via KFGO](https://kfgo.com/2026/08/19/bonds-steady-after-us-treasury-comes-to-the-rescue/) (accessed 2026-08-20, Established, confidence: High for the Reuters byline)). Energy equities outperformed technology on the 18 August selloff: Reuters wrote that fading hopes for a U.S.–Iran peace deal “sustained gains in oil prices and kept government bond yields at multi-year peaks,” that “the developments pushed Brent crude futures up 0.5% at around three-week highs,” and that “the S&P 500 Energy sector gained 1.4%” while “the S&P 500 Information Technology sector was the biggest drag, down 1.5%,” with Nvidia down 2% and Meta down 3% ([Reuters via MarketScreener](https://www.marketscreener.com/news/tech-stocks-weigh-on-wall-street-as-iran-stalemate-lifts-oil-yields-ce7859ddd88ef724) (accessed 2026-08-20, Established, confidence: High)). Kalkine’s 18 August sector table (Low-tier relative to wires) had Energy (XLE) +1.76% and Technology (XLK) −2.47% ([Kalkine](https://kalkine.com/news/premium/us-sector-performance-analysis-energy-and-health-care-lead-as-technology-retreats) (accessed 2026-08-20, Low, confidence: Low until corroborated by a primary index vendor)).

Wires explicitly described the oil → inflation-worry → long-yield → tech-multiple channel on 18 August, which is the session when the Nasdaq-100 fell most in this window. FRED records the Nasdaq-100 close at 29,995.38 on 17 August, 29,490.96 on 18 August (−1.68%), and 29,426.02 on 19 August (−0.22%) ([FRED NASDAQ100](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/NASDAQ100) (accessed 2026-08-20, **Primary**, confidence: High)). Reuters’ 18 August stock wrap said indexes fell to about two-week lows, “pressured by losses in heavyweight technology stocks, while fading hopes for a U.S.–Iran peace deal sustained gains in oil prices and kept government bond yields at multi-year peaks”; that the 30-year Treasury yield “stood at its highest since 2007”; and that “most megacap and growth stocks declined, as high government bond yields potentially lower the present value of future tech profits and increase corporate borrowing costs” ([Reuters via MarketScreener](https://www.marketscreener.com/news/tech-stocks-weigh-on-wall-street-as-iran-stalemate-lifts-oil-yields-ce7859ddd88ef724)). Kim Forrest of Bokeh Capital told Reuters: “The yields are troubling people because it portends a tighter environment and it’s going to be more expensive to borrow money. Especially in this whole AI thing where time to pay it back is uncertain” (same Reuters wrap). The same story noted that “the benchmark S&P 500 closed lower in the previous session, backing away from record highs as higher crude oil prices revived inflation worries.” On 19 August, Sam Stovall of CFRA told Reuters: “Inflation does remain a concern and elevated oil prices are contributing to that. If the Federal Reserve is not going to do anything about it, the bond market will” (Reuters 19 August wrap, 12:18 p.m. ET snapshot, via search snippet of MarketScreener; full page 401/access denied, confidence: Medium). CNBC’s 20 August Daily Open listed “Inflation, the Iran war, alongside the massive debt” as hanging over the economy after Treasury doubled long-end buybacks ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/20/cnbc-daily-open-treasury-us-debt-moderna-cancer.html) (accessed 2026-08-20, Established, confidence: High)).

That transmission should not be flattened into “oil raised inflation breakevens, which raised nominal yields, which cut tech multiples,” because the Federal Reserve’s own intermeeting account is more precise and partly cuts against the breakeven leg. The 28–29 July FOMC minutes, posted with last update 19 August 2026, state: “developments over the intermeeting period were influenced by the conflict in the Middle East. Oil prices ended the period higher following the escalation of tensions in the Middle East. Across asset classes, inflation compensation moved little in response to higher oil prices, nominal rates rose largely on expectations of higher policy rates, equities were somewhat lower, and the dollar edged up modestly.” They add that near-term inflation compensation “moved up only marginally thereafter despite the sharp increase in oil prices,” and that “market-based measures of longer-term inflation compensation and survey-based measures of inflation expectations remained well anchored,” while “nominal Treasury yields rose 25 to 30 basis points, driven by corresponding increases in real interest rates” ([Federal Reserve, FOMC minutes 28–29 July 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcminutes20260729.htm) (accessed 2026-08-20, **Primary**, confidence: High)). Staff attributed elevated May PCE (4.1% total, 3.4% core) in part to “higher energy and input costs stemming from the conflict in the Middle East.” Participants “judged that their inflation outlooks were highly uncertain and that inflation risks were skewed to the upside. Many participants noted that the recent re-escalation of the conflict in the Middle East significantly clouded the inflation outlook” and that “a protracted conflict could prolong supply chain challenges and could put upward pressures on inflation” (same minutes). AP’s minutes wrap said Fed officials “worried that the Iran war, tariffs, and heavy investment in AI infrastructure were all boosting prices,” and that inflation had since shown “some signs of cooling, though gas prices have rebounded this month on renewed hostilities in the Middle East” ([AP via KPC News](https://www.kpcnews.com/article_b5aa8fb0-1eb3-5751-ac07-17826bb0f9fa.html) (accessed 2026-08-20, Established, confidence: High)). This suggests the August 18 tech move is better described as oil-and-Hormuz risk raising the odds of tighter policy and higher real/term yields, which then compressed duration-sensitive multiples, rather than as a clean jump in inflation expectations.

The July policy decision itself was a 9–3 hold at 3½ to 3¾ percent. The 29 July statement said activity was expanding “despite elevated uncertainty that owes, in part, to the conflict in the Middle East” and that “inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee’s 2 percent goal, in part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors, including energy.” Voting against were Beth M. Hammack, Neel Kashkari, and Lorie K. Logan, “who preferred to raise the target range for the federal funds rate by 1/4 percentage point at this meeting” ([Federal Reserve, 29 July 2026 FOMC statement](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260729a.htm) (accessed 2026-08-20, **Primary**, confidence: High); names and 9–3 tally restated in the minutes). On the policy outlook, the minutes say: “Many participants assessed that policy tightening would likely be necessary if inflation did not decline.” “Several participants favored an increase of 25 basis points” at the meeting itself. CNBC quoted those sentences in its 19 August minutes story ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/19/fed-minutes-july-2026-officials-saw-need-for-rate-hike-if-inflation-doesnt-cool.html) (accessed 2026-08-20, Established, confidence: High)). Quartz, citing the minutes, added that participants cited “tariff pass-through, energy costs tied to the conflict in the Middle East, and demand generated by the AI buildout” ([Quartz](https://qz.com/fed-fomc-minutes-july-2026-rate-hike-dissent-081926) (accessed 2026-08-20, Established, confidence: High)).

Whether those hawkish minutes *caused* the Nasdaq-100 to keep falling on 19 August after the Treasury buyback bounce is not established as a single-cause fact. Minutes were released Wednesday, 19 August; a secondary recap states the standard 2:00 p.m. ET time ([GoldSilver.com](https://goldsilver.com/industry-news/goldsilver-news/fomc-minutes-warsh-six-meetings-gold-reaction/) (accessed 2026-08-20, Low for the clock time, confidence: Low); CNBC and AP say only “Wednesday.”). Treasury’s same-day announcement that it would at least double long-end liquidity-support buybacks, from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation effective 9 September through 4 November, sent the 10-year yield down about 6 basis points to around 4.65% and the 30-year down about 9 basis points to around 5.20% ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/19/treasury-announces-upscaled-buyback-operation-for-longer-term-debt-sending-yields-lower.html); [CNBC Daily Open](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/20/cnbc-daily-open-treasury-us-debt-moderna-cancer.html)). Reuters at 12:18 p.m. ET—before a 2 p.m. minutes print—had the Nasdaq Composite up 0.59% (search snippet of the 19 August wrap). By the close, FRED shows the Nasdaq-100 still down 0.22% on the day, while Barchart reports the S&P 500 +0.21%, the Dow +0.22%, and the Nasdaq-100 −0.22% at “a 1.5-week low,” with “weakness in chipmaker and AI-infrastructure stocks continued for a second day” and the minutes characterized as “slightly hawkish” because “many Fed officials indicated that policy tightening would be necessary if inflation didn’t decline” and the Iran re-escalation “clouded the inflation outlook” ([Barchart](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/3937543/stocks-finish-mixed-as-bond-yields-fall-and-chipmakers-slide) (accessed 2026-08-20, Established, confidence: High for the close split)). HDFC Sky, a secondary wrap, wrote that all three major averages had earlier rallied more than 0.6% “but gains were pared as investors weighed hawkish undertones from the Federal Reserve’s July meeting minutes released in the afternoon,” with the Nasdaq Composite still closing +0.16% at 26,331.09 ([HDFC Sky](https://hdfcsky.com/news/dow-sp-500-nasdaq-snap-3-day-losing-streak-as-treasury-doubles-debt-buybacks-to-curb-yields-august-20-2026) (accessed 2026-08-20, Low, confidence: Low for the intraday 0.6% figure)). CNBC’s 20 August Daily Open said the buyback “helped snap a three-day losing streak for stocks, with all three major U.S. indexes rising about 0.2%,” which matches the Composite/S&P/Dow close and does not match the Nasdaq-100 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/20/cnbc-daily-open-treasury-us-debt-moderna-cancer.html)). Reuters on 20 August treated the minutes as background (“concern about inflation deepened, with ‘several’ policymakers appearing ready to raise interest rates and ‘many’ saying a hike … would be needed if inflation does not decline”) while attributing Thursday’s sour sentiment to fading buyback relief and “elevated oil prices,” with Brent at $93.06 ([Reuters via KFGO](https://kfgo.com/2026/08/19/bonds-steady-after-us-treasury-comes-to-the-rescue/)). The sourced pattern is: buybacks dominated the long-end yield drop; the Nasdaq-100 did not confirm that bounce and closed slightly lower; chip/AI-infrastructure selling was already a second-day story; hawkish minutes are cited as a reason earlier equity gains were pared, not as a standalone dump that reversed an otherwise healed NDX. Isolating the minutes from concurrent chip selling, oil, and a weak 20-year auction (Barchart: bid-to-cover 2.53 vs 10-auction average 2.64) is not possible from these wraps.

Jackson Hole is a positioning event, not a realized 1–20 August catalyst. The Kansas City Fed states that the 2026 Jackson Hole Economic Policy Symposium “will take place Aug. 27-29” on the topic “Financial Innovation: Implications for Payments and Policy” ([Kansas City Fed](https://www.kansascityfed.org/research/jackson-hole-economic-symposium/) (accessed 2026-08-20 via search snippet, **Primary**, confidence: High for dates/theme; full page timed out)). At the 29 July press conference, Chair Kevin Warsh described the August speech as “a blank piece of paper right now,” said he had not decided whether it would be “a big-picture speech” or “a more traditional set up for all the action we’re going to have between September and December,” and said he wanted, in Jackson, to discuss “big questions” including productivity, demographics, and “the global economy amid the shocks” ([Federal Reserve, Warsh press conference transcript, 29 July 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/mediacenter/files/FOMCpresconf20260729.pdf) (accessed 2026-08-20, **Primary**, confidence: High)). No source reviewed for this axis treats Jackson Hole as having already moved 1–20 August tech prices.

Housing starts and import prices printed at 8:30 a.m. ET on 18 August, the same day as the large tech decline, but established 18 August equity wraps did not cite them as the tech driver. Census/HUD reported July housing starts at a 1,239,000 seasonally adjusted annual rate, 12.4% below June’s 1,415,000 ([Census Bureau, New Residential Construction, 18 August 2026](https://www.census.gov/construction/nrc/current/index.html) (accessed 2026-08-20, **Primary**, confidence: High)). BLS reported July import prices −0.4% month-over-month after −0.3% in June, with fuel imports −7.2% more than offsetting higher nonfuel prices, and imports still +5.9% year-over-year ([BLS, U.S. Import and Export Price Indexes, 18 August 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ximpim.htm) (accessed 2026-08-20, **Primary**, confidence: High)). Reuters’ 18 August tech wrap attributed the Nasdaq/IT decline to the Iran stalemate, oil, and multi-year-peak yields, and mentioned Wednesday’s Fed minutes as a later clue; it did not mention housing starts or import prices ([Reuters via MarketScreener](https://www.marketscreener.com/news/tech-stocks-weigh-on-wall-street-as-iran-stalemate-lifts-oil-yields-ce7859ddd88ef724)). A cooler-than-expected import-price print would, if anything, have been a mild disinflation signal for that session; year-over-year import prices remaining high is a separate, slower-moving fact. This supports the prior-research conclusion that 18 August housing starts and import prices were not cited as drivers of the tech selloff.

## Key Findings

The 17 August MoU expiry and Trump’s 18 August “no talks” / blockade-in-force posts coincided with the largest one-day Nasdaq-100 drop in this window (−1.68% on 18 August), and Reuters explicitly tied that session’s tech losses to the Iran stalemate, firmer oil, and multi-year-high long yields. Tracked Hormuz traffic was in the single digits versus ~130 ships a day before the February war, even though Trump claimed the strait was open. Energy outperformed technology on 18 August (S&P Energy +1.4%, IT −1.5% per Reuters). The July FOMC, on the record, treated Middle East energy shocks as an upside inflation risk and a reason a 9–3 majority still held while “many” participants said tightening would likely be needed if inflation did not decline; those minutes, released 19 August, were described as slightly hawkish and as a reason a buyback-led bounce was pared, but they did not produce a broad index reversal and cannot be isolated from ongoing chip/AI-infrastructure selling. Housing starts and import prices on 18 August were not cited by the 18 August Reuters equity wrap as the tech driver.

## Risks and Uncertainties

Daily Hormuz counts and oil prints disagree at the margin; inflation-compensation evidence from July does not fully map onto the 18–19 August tape; 19 August NDX weakness has multiple concurrent explanations. Direct Kpler and some Reuters pages were not retrievable.
