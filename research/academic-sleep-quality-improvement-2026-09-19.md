## Literature Overview

### Foundational Works

How researchers and clinicians decide what “improves adult sleep quality” is still shaped by a small set of mid-to-late twentieth-century models and instruments. According to [Borbély (1982)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/7185792) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High), human sleep propensity is generated by the interaction of a sleep-dependent homeostatic process (Process S) and a sleep-independent circadian process (Process C). [Daan, Beersma, and Borbély (1984)](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.1984.246.2.R161) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) then formalized the same idea as a quantitative timing model. In a later reappraisal, [Borbély, Daan, Wirz-Justice, and Deboer (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.12371) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) stated that the two-process framework had remained the field’s major conceptual model for more than three decades. This still matters for choosing improvement methods because it separates two failure modes: insufficient homeostatic sleep pressure versus circadian misalignment. This suggests that sleep-restriction and regular rise-time rules target Process S, whereas light timing and schedule realignment target Process C, and that “more time in bed” is not automatically restorative.

The clinical counterpart for chronic insomnia is the three-factor, or 3P, model. According to [Spielman, Caruso, and Glovinsky (1987)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0193-953X(18)30532-X) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High), insomnia unfolds from predisposing vulnerability, precipitating events, and perpetuating behaviors that keep the problem going after the original trigger has faded. The same authors’ companion trial, [Spielman, Saskin, and Thorpy (1987)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3563247/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High), established sleep-restriction therapy as a formal protocol: time in bed was set to estimated average sleep time (not less than 4.5 hours), then titrated by subjective sleep efficiency. Stimulus control supplied the other behavioral pillar. According to [Bootzin (1972)](https://www.med.upenn.edu/cbti/assets/user-content/documents/Bootzin%201972.pdf) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High), falling asleep is an operant response that should occur only in the presence of sleep-compatible cues. Later elaborations added a fixed morning rise time and a no-napping rule, as summarized in a recent systematic review ([Jansson-Fröjmark et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14002)) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High). This suggests that methods aimed at “sleep quality” in people who spend long, frustrated nights in bed are often really methods for restoring the bed as a cue for sleep, not for increasing sleep opportunity.

The modern clinical term “sleep hygiene” is generally credited to Peter Hauri’s 1977 Upjohn monograph *Current Concepts: The Sleep Disorders* ([Wellcome Collection record](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/k2b8eke4)) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High). According to [Stepanski and Wyatt (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1053/smrv.2001.0246) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High), Hauri’s original rules mixed experimentally supported items with clinical observations, and they found no adequate empirical demonstration that a comprehensive hygiene package, used alone, treats insomnia. [Morin, Culbert, and Schwartz (1994)](https://doi.org/10.1176/ajp.151.8.1172) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) found stimulus control and sleep restriction the most effective single procedures and sleep-hygiene education ineffective when used alone. The 2021 American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) guideline now gives a conditional recommendation *against* sleep hygiene as monotherapy for chronic insomnia ([Edinger et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8986)) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High). This suggests that hygiene remains useful as education, but it is a weak choice if the goal is to change adult sleep quality in clinical insomnia.

Cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) assembled these pieces. [Morin (1993)](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL8270474M/Insomnia) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) published the first widely used treatment manual; the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) first appeared there. The first peer-reviewed ISI validation followed in [Bastien, Vallières, and Morin (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1389-9457(00)00065-4) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High). Two landmark randomized trials then showed that the integrated package worked. [Morin, Colecchi, Stone, Sood, and Brink (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.281.11.991) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) compared eight weeks of CBT, temazepam, both, and placebo in 78 older adults: all three active treatments beat placebo, but CBT gains were better sustained at 3-, 12-, and 24-month follow-up. [Edinger, Wohlgemuth, Radtke, Marsh, and Quillian (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.285.14.1856) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) then showed that a six-week hybrid of sleep education, stimulus control, and time-in-bed restriction outperformed both progressive muscle relaxation and a placebo on sleep logs. [Harvey (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0005-7967(01)00061-4) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) added an explicit cognitive maintenance model: excessive worry about sleep produces arousal, selective attention, overestimation of deficits, and safety behaviors. This still matters because it explains why purely educational hygiene often fails and why an intervention can “work” on worry and satisfaction even when total sleep time barely changes.

What “works” depends on how sleep quality is defined. According to [Krystal and Edinger (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1389-9457(08)70011-X) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High), “sleep quality” is a widely used term without a clear definition. The most-cited subjective instrument remains the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. According to [Buysse, Reynolds, Monk, Berman, and Kupfer (1989)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-1781(89)90047-4) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High), the PSQI is a 19-item questionnaire whose seven components sum to a global score; a cutoff greater than 5 distinguished good from poor sleepers with 89.6% sensitivity and 86.5% specificity in the original sample. The National Sleep Foundation’s later expert panel reached consensus that continuity variables—sleep latency, awakenings longer than five minutes, wake after sleep onset, and sleep efficiency—were appropriate indicators of good sleep quality across the lifespan, with less consensus on sleep architecture ([Ohayon et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2016.11.006)) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High). The 2015 AASM/Sleep Research Society adult consensus recommended seven or more hours of sleep per night for adults aged 18–60 while noting that healthy sleep also requires quality, timing, regularity, and the absence of sleep disorders ([Watson et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.4950)) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High). [Buysse (2014)](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.3298) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) defined sleep health as a multidimensional pattern that includes subjective satisfaction, timing, duration, efficiency, and daytime alertness.

Early quantitative reviews already pointed toward the methods that later became first-line care. In 59 group studies and 2,102 patients, [Morin et al. (1994)](https://doi.org/10.1176/ajp.151.8.1172) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) reported average effect sizes of 0.88 for sleep latency and 0.65 for wake after sleep onset after psychological therapy. The 2005 NIH State-of-the-Science conference concluded that CBT packages were as effective as prescription hypnotics for short-term chronic insomnia and that benefits appeared to last beyond the end of treatment unlike medications ([NIH, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/28.9.1049)) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High). Later society guidelines locked that conversion in: the American College of Physicians recommended CBT-I as initial treatment for all adults with chronic insomnia ([Qaseem et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.7326/M15-2175)) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); the European Sleep Research Society recommended CBT-I as first-line treatment ([Riemann et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.12594)) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); and AASM 2021 strongly recommended multicomponent CBT-I and conditionally recommended against standalone sleep hygiene ([Edinger et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8986)) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High).

> Conflicts noted: The field credits Hauri (1977) with modern “sleep hygiene,” but Gigli and Zucconi (2013) argue the concept should be antedated to Mantegazza (1864). “Sleep quality” has no single official AASM scored construct: NSF 2017 emphasizes continuity metrics, Buysse 2014 treats satisfaction as one of five sleep-health dimensions, and the PSQI folds duration, medication, and daytime function into one global score. Subjective–objective discrepancy is variously framed as misperception, a distinct subtype, or a limitation of standard PSG.
>

No sources found for a peer-reviewed journal version of Hauri’s 1977 hygiene list. No independently verified Google Scholar citation counts were treated as primary facts.

### Recent Advances (last 2–3 years)

The most consequential guideline change in this window is the AASM combination-treatment clinical practice guideline, published 13 April 2026. According to the AASM, the academy issues a *conditional* suggestion for concurrent CBT-I plus insomnia medication over medication alone, and a *conditional* suggestion *against* combination therapy over CBT-I alone, both at low certainty of evidence ([AASM news release](https://aasm.org/combination-treatment-chronic-insomnia-guideline/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Buysse et al., *J Clin Sleep Med* 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13076838/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The companion GRADE systematic review found that versus medication alone, combination therapy improved global insomnia severity by a re-expressed 2.5 PSQI points and diary sleep continuity, while diary total sleep time did not meet the pre-specified clinically meaningful threshold. Versus CBT-I alone, global severity and continuity did not meet those thresholds, though diary total sleep time was +13.8 minutes ([Buysse et al. systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44470-025-00039-7) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). This suggests that adding a hypnotic to CBT-I can buy a little more sleep time, but not a more meaningful improvement in perceived insomnia quality than CBT-I alone.

That pattern is corroborated by a 2024 network meta-analysis of 13 trials in 823 hypnotic-free adults: starting with CBT-I produced higher long-term remission than pharmacotherapy (odds ratio 1.82), translating to about 41% versus 28% remission, with no clear advantage of combination over CBT-I ([Furukawa et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/pcn.13730) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). A much larger component network meta-analysis (241 trials; 31,452 adults) identified cognitive restructuring, third-wave elements, sleep restriction, and stimulus control as the active ingredients, found sleep-hygiene education non-essential (incremental OR 1.01), and reported that relaxation was potentially counterproductive; the best in-person package had a remission risk difference of 0.33 and number needed to treat of 3.0 ([Furukawa et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2023.5060) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High for direction; Low for the exact NNT as a single-source number)).

The European Sleep Research Society 2023 European Insomnia Guideline remains the current ESRS treatment guideline. According to [Riemann et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High), CBT-I is first-line for chronic insomnia at any adult age, in-person or digital (grade A); pharmacotherapy may follow if CBT-I is insufficient; benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, and daridorexant may be used ≤4 weeks; prolonged-release melatonin may be used up to 3 months in people ≥55 years (B); antihistamines, antipsychotics, fast-release melatonin, ramelteon, and phytotherapeutics are not recommended (A); light therapy and exercise are adjuncts to CBT-I only (B). NICE did not issue a new standalone insomnia clinical guideline in 2024–2026. Technology appraisal TA922 recommends daridorexant for adults with chronic insomnia and considerable daytime impairment only if CBT-I has failed or is unavailable ([NICE TA922](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ta922/chapter/1-Recommendations) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Sleepio remains recommended as a cost-saving primary-care option versus sleep hygiene or sleeping pills ([NICE HTG624](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/htg624/chapter/1-Recommendations) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

Digital CBT-I is the best-replicated recent advance for sleep quality, not merely duration. A 2025 meta-analysis of 29 fully automated dCBT-I trials (n = 9,475) reported a moderate-to-large post-treatment effect on insomnia severity (SMD −0.71), small-to-moderate diary effects for sleep efficiency, latency, and wake after sleep onset, and only a small total-sleep-time effect (SMD 0.19); therapist-supported CBT-I outperformed fully automated programs ([Hwang et al., 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01514-4) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). A 15-trial head-to-head equivalence meta-analysis found eHealth and in-person CBT-I statistically equivalent on most outcomes, including PSQI ([JMIR Ment Health 2024](https://mental.jmir.org/2024/1/e58217/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). This suggests that digital CBT-I reliably improves perceived insomnia severity and continuity; total sleep time gains are smaller and slower; effects look largest against waitlist and shrink against active or usual-care controls.

Exercise evidence has grown faster than timing evidence. A 2025 network meta-analysis of 22 RCTs (1,348 participants) reported that yoga increased diary total sleep time and walking/jogging reduced ISI, though certainty was often low and some point estimates are unusually large ([BMJ Evid Based Med](https://ebm.bmj.com/content/early/2025/07/09/bmjebm-2024-113512) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High for direction; Low for the largest point estimates)). A 12-week RCT in 58 sedentary men found both morning and evening aerobic exercise shortened sleep latency without changing sleep duration; only morning exercise advanced dim-light melatonin onset ([Sci Rep 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-02659-8) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). This suggests that exercise improves sleep quality; timing mainly shifts circadian phase rather than determining whether quality improves.

On substances, recent meta-analyses are clearer for caffeine and alcohol than for nicotine as a treatment target. Caffeine across 24 studies reduced total sleep time by 45 minutes and sleep efficiency by 7%, and increased latency by 9 minutes and wake after sleep onset by 12 minutes; modeled cut-offs were about 8.8 hours before bed for a 107 mg coffee ([Gardiner et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Alcohol across 27 studies delayed REM onset and reduced REM duration even at about two drinks, while high doses shortened sleep-onset latency; effects on total sleep time, efficiency, and wake after sleep onset were too uncertain to estimate ([Gardiner et al., 2024/2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). No sources found for large 2023–2026 RCTs of nicotine cessation as a sleep-quality intervention in community adults.

Melatonin remains the sharpest effect-size conflict. A 2023 insomnia-disorder meta-analysis (22 studies; 4,875 participants) found prolonged-release melatonin shortened subjective sleep-onset latency by 6.30 minutes and objective latency by 5.05 minutes and raised objective sleep efficiency by 1.91% ([Maruani et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13939) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). ESRS, reviewing the same literature, does *not* recommend fast-release melatonin or ramelteon for insomnia absent circadian indications, and allows prolonged-release melatonin only in people ≥55 ([Riemann et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). A 2025 RCT of magnesium bisglycinate 250 mg elemental (n = 155 adults with poor sleep) reduced ISI by 3.9 versus 2.3 points (Cohen *d* = 0.2)—statistically significant but below usual 6–8 point response thresholds ([DOI 10.2147/nss.s524348](https://doi.org/10.2147/nss.s524348) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). A 2026 systematic review of 12 magnesium RCTs concluded that current evidence does not support routine magnesium for insomnia ([DOI 10.1080/19390211.2026.2719670](https://doi.org/10.1080/19390211.2026.2719670) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

Dual orexin receptor antagonists (DORAs) are the main hypnotic update. A 2025 network meta-analysis of eight trials (n = 5,198) found daridorexant 25/50 mg, lemborexant 5/10 mg, and suvorexant 20/15 mg all beat placebo on subjective latency, total sleep time, wake after sleep onset, and ISI ([Kishi et al., 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41398-025-03439-8) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Mindfulness effects are moderate and often smaller than CBT-I. A 2024/2025 meta-regression of 40 mindfulness-based intervention RCTs reported a sleep-disturbance SMD of −0.52 ([DOI 10.1080/15402002.2024.2401457](https://doi.org/10.1080/15402002.2024.2401457) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Wearable and closed-loop devices remain mixed: one 2024 peer-reviewed crossover in 21 adults found alpha-phase-locked auditory stimulation reduced weekly sleep-onset latency by 10.5 minutes versus silent sham ([Sci Rep 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-63385-1) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)), while ESRS 2023 advised against unvalidated sleep trackers in insomnia until PSG validation ([Riemann et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

> Conflicts noted: Melatonin effect sizes disagree across Maruani 2023, a 2025 scoping review, and ESRS 2023. Digital versus in-person CBT-I is another split: ESRS 2023 grades digital CBT-I as first-line, NICE still asks for more Sleepio-versus-face-to-face data, and Hwang 2025 found therapist-supported programs superior to fully automated ones. Exercise network meta-analyses report very large yoga and walking effects that sit well above typical trial magnitudes. Combination therapy increases total sleep time versus CBT-I in AASM analyses but does not improve ISI/PSQI versus CBT-I alone.
>
> Gaps: No sources found for a 2024–2026 ESRS insomnia guideline after the 2023 update. No sources found for a new NICE clinical insomnia guideline. No sources found for a living Cochrane review of CBT-I in general adult insomnia (the 2025 Cochrane update is cancer-specific). No sources found for large 2023–2026 RCTs of nicotine cessation, alcohol reduction, or bedroom-temperature prescriptions with PSQI/ISI as primary outcomes in general adults.

### Survey Papers and Reviews

The highest-leverage summaries of what improves adult sleep quality are clinical practice guidelines and large systematic reviews rather than single trials. Four guideline families dominate 2016–2026 practice: the AASM behavioral guideline (2021) and pharmacologic guideline (2017); the American College of Physicians 2016 chronic-insomnia guideline; the ESRS European Insomnia Guideline (2023 update); and NICE-linked UK guidance ([Edinger et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Sateia et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263087/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Qaseem et al., 2016](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M15-2175) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Riemann et al., 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jsr.14035) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

AASM’s 2021 guideline, based on 66 RCTs of CBT-I versus control, gives the only **strong** recommendation among behavioral treatments: clinicians should use multicomponent CBT-I for chronic insomnia in adults. Conditional recommendations favor brief therapies, stimulus control, sleep restriction therapy, and relaxation as single-component options. AASM made no recommendation for standalone mindfulness, cognitive therapy, biofeedback, paradoxical intention, or intensive sleep retraining because fewer than three eligible studies met inclusion ([Edinger et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Trauer and colleagues’ 2015 *Annals* meta-analysis of 20 face-to-face multimodal CBT-I RCTs (1,162 adults) reported diary sleep-onset latency improved by 19.03 minutes, wake after sleep onset by 26.00 minutes, and sleep efficiency by 9.91 percentage points; total sleep time improved by a non-significant 7.61 minutes ([Trauer et al., 2015](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M14-2841) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High for direction; Low for the exact minute-level estimates as single-source numbers)).

AASM gives a **conditional recommendation against** sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy: only two low-quality RCTs showed a clinically meaningful improvement on one critical outcome, and offering hygiene alone can delay effective care ([Edinger et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Chung and colleagues (2018) meta-analyzed 15 RCTs and found CBT-I superior to hygiene by about 8 percentage points in diary sleep efficiency and about 2 PSQI points ([Chung et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/fampra/cmx122) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

Xie and colleagues (2021) meta-analyzed 22 RCTs of regular exercise in adults without “pathological diseases.” Versus control, exercise lowered PSQI (MD −2.19), ISI (MD −1.52), and Epworth Sleepiness Scale (MD −2.55). Physical and mind–body exercise improved subjective sleep similarly; actigraphic effects were mostly null except sleep efficiency ([Xie et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2021.664499/full) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High for direction)). ESRS 2023: exercise is not recommended as standalone insomnia therapy, but may be useful as an adjunct to CBT-I ([Riemann et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

AASM 2017, based on 2 mg trials, suggests clinicians not use melatonin for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia (weak recommendation; insufficient/low-quality evidence) and also recommends against valerian, diphenhydramine, trazodone, tryptophan, and tiagabine ([Sateia et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263087/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). ESRS 2023 does not recommend fast-release melatonin or ramelteon for insomnia unless circadian factors are involved; prolonged-release melatonin may be considered for up to 3 months in adults ≥55 years ([Riemann et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Mah and Pitre (2021) pooled three placebo RCTs in 151 older adults and concluded the magnesium literature is “substandard” for clinical recommendations ([Mah & Pitre, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-021-03297-z) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). A 2026 systematic review of 12 RCTs concluded current evidence does not support oral magnesium as a routine insomnia treatment ([DOI 10.1080/19390211.2026.2719670](https://doi.org/10.1080/19390211.2026.2719670) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). ESRS 2023: herbal remedies including valerian are not recommended because of insufficient evidence ([Riemann et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

AASM 2021 issued no recommendation for mindfulness therapies. Rusch and colleagues (2019; 18 RCTs) found mindfulness no better than specific active sleep treatments but better than nonspecific active controls ([Rusch et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30575050) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: Medium)). A 2024 review of weighted blankets in psychiatric samples reported mixed insomnia results and stressed few well-designed RCTs ([Journal of Psychiatric Research 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2024.09.027) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: Medium)). A 2025 meta-analysis of three double-blind crossover RCTs of evening amber lenses found no significant effects on latency, total sleep time, efficiency, or wake after sleep onset ([Luna-Rangel et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2025.1699303/full) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). A 2024 *Sleep Medicine Reviews* synthesis found hotter conditions associated with shorter and more disrupted sleep, but the authors explicitly called for interventional trials of cooling ([Chevance et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.101915) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). No sources found for an AASM or ESRS recommendation of blue-blocking glasses or a target bedroom temperature as insomnia treatment.

De Crescenzo and colleagues’ 2022 *Lancet* network meta-analysis (154 double-blind RCTs; 44,089 participants) found acute efficacy versus placebo for benzodiazepines, doxylamine, eszopiclone, lemborexant, seltorexant, zolpidem, and zopiclone; long-term efficacy data mainly supported eszopiclone and lemborexant at very low certainty ([De Crescenzo et al., 2022](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736%2822%2900878-9/fulltext) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). NICE CKS: do not recommend OTC antihistamine sleep aids; avoid long-term benzodiazepines/Z-drugs ([NICE CKS insomnia](https://cks.nice.org.uk/topics/insomnia/management/managing-insomnia/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

This suggests the following evidence ranking for methods. Multicomponent CBT-I, including digital delivery, is the only strongly recommended first-line method. Core CBT-I components (sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring) rank next. Exercise has a replicated subjective benefit as an adjunct. Prescription hypnotics and orexin antagonists have established acute efficacy as short-term second-line care. Prolonged-release melatonin in adults ≥55 is modest and guideline-split. Mindfulness is not a CBT-I replacement. Sleep hygiene alone is not recommended as a treatment. Magnesium, valerian, weighted blankets, blue-blocking glasses, and a prescribed bedroom temperature have insufficient evidence or guideline language against them.

> Conflicts noted: Melatonin is the clearest transatlantic split: AASM 2017 weakly recommends against melatonin for chronic insomnia, while ESRS 2023 and NICE CKS allow prolonged-release melatonin in adults ≥55. Sleep hygiene is offered in UK primary-care pathways yet not recommended as standalone therapy by AASM and ESRS. Hypnotic hierarchies differ across AASM, the *Lancet* 2022 network, NICE (daridorexant after CBT-I failure), and ESRS. OTC antihistamines are not recommended by AASM, ESRS, and NICE even though the *Lancet* network found acute efficacy for doxylamine.
>
> Gaps: No completed Cochrane systematic review of CBT-I for working-age adults. No Cochrane review of melatonin, magnesium, valerian, weighted blankets, blue-light blocking, or bedroom-temperature interventions for primary adult insomnia. Weighted-blanket reviews are confined to psychiatric samples. Head-to-head ranking of consumer methods against CBT-I in the same network does not exist in the sources opened.

## Citation Landscape

### Citation Clusters and Sub-communities

This map is qualitative. No original co-citation matrix was computed for this report. Cluster membership is inferred from published Web of Science bibliometric papers, society guideline reference lists, and who cites whom in reviews ([Xin et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9520059/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Wan et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9845786/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [He et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2023.1184302/full) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Semantic Scholar live citation totals could not be verified on the access date because a key paper page timed out. No field-wide Connected Papers map covering all axes was found.

Six intellectual camps are visible. They share some authors and journals, but they do not share a single outcome language. The camps are: CBT-I / behavioral sleep medicine; sleep-quality measurement; circadian / light / melatonin; hypnotic and orexin pharmacology; public-health sleep duration / “sleep health”; and a younger consumer-wearable validation literature.

A Web of Science bibliometric of 736 CBT-I papers (2004–2021) found the densest venues to be *Sleep*, *Behavioral Sleep Medicine*, *Sleep Medicine*, *Sleep Medicine Reviews*, and *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine*. The most prolific authors were Charles Morin and Colin Espie; the leading institutions were Université Laval and the University of Pennsylvania ([Xin et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9520059/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). In that corpus, the most-cited papers included Morin’s 2006 evidence update, the 2016 ACP guideline, Morin’s 2009 *JAMA* CBT-I-plus-medication trial, Trauer’s 2015 *Annals* meta-analysis, and Sivertsen’s 2006 CBT-I-versus-zopiclone *JAMA* trial. The 2021 AASM review treats diary-reported sleep quality, sleep latency, wake after sleep onset, remission, and responder rate as critical outcomes; a 30-study ISI meta-analysis in that review favored CBT-I (effect size 0.95) ([Edinger et al., 2021 systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853211/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). This suggests that the CBT-I camp defines “better sleep” as a lower ISI, higher diary sleep efficiency, and clinical remission—not a PSG architecture change and not a wearable score.

The measurement camp is the shared toolbox. Bastien, Vallières, and Morin’s 2001 ISI validation was the single most-cited insomnia paper in Wan and colleagues’ Web of Science top-100 list, with 3,384 citations as of their 24 March 2022 search ([Wan et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9845786/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Those citation totals are WoS counts at one 2022 search date; they are not co-citation frequencies. The Consensus Sleep Diary was produced by a Pittsburgh Assessment Conference working group to standardize prospective sleep-onset latency, wake after sleep onset, total sleep time, and sleep efficiency ([Carney et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3250369/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The European insomnia guideline states that the PSQI “is not a specific instrument for diagnosing insomnia and should not be used for that purpose,” while treating ISI as a severity and case-detection tool ([Riemann et al., 2017](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jsr.12594) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Criterion-validity work has long shown weak PSQI–PSG correlations ([Grandner et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3399671/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). This suggests that questionnaires and objective methods frequently diverge, and that divergence is itself a recurring citation theme.

A CiteSpace analysis of 4,696 WoS papers on insomnia and circadian rhythm reported high-frequency keywords of circadian rhythms, sleep, insomnia, light, and melatonin ([He et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2023.1184302/full) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Foundational physiology papers include Lewy et al. (1980) on light suppressing melatonin and Czeisler et al. (1986) on bright light resetting the pacemaker ([Lewy et al., 1980](https://cet.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Lewy-1980-Science.pdf) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Czeisler et al., 1986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3726555/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The 2015 AASM intrinsic circadian-rhythm guideline organizes this camp’s treatment citations: strategically timed melatonin for delayed sleep-wake phase disorder and for blind adults with non-24-hour sleep-wake disorder ([Auger et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4582061/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Success here is phase alignment—a shifted dim-light melatonin onset and a corrected schedule—not an ISI drop per se.

The pharmacology camp’s molecular roots are the 1998 hypocretin/orexin discovery papers ([de Lecea et al., 1998](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.95.1.322) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Sakurai et al., 1998](https://www.cell.com/fulltext/S0092-8674(00)80949-6) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The 2017 AASM pharmacologic guideline is the clinical citation hub; suvorexant evidence was read largely from PSG wake after sleep onset and latency to persistent sleep ([Sateia et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263087/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). “Better sleep” here is a PSG change plus a matching diary change, with next-day residual effects as the harm language.

Public-health sleep research defines sleep health as a multidimensional pattern that promotes well-being, characterized by satisfaction, timing, duration, efficiency, and alertness ([Buysse, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3902880/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Duration-as-exposure papers include Cappuccio and colleagues’ 2010 meta-analysis of a U-shaped mortality association ([Cappuccio et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20469800/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Consensus statements then converted that epidemiology into a public number: AASM/SRS recommended ≥7 hours for adults aged 18–60 ([Watson et al., 2015](https://aasm.org/resources/pdf/adultsleepdurationconsensus.pdf) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). “Better sleep” in this camp is enough hours on a regular schedule, not an 8-point ISI drop.

The consumer-wearable cluster is real but younger. Its organizing documents are the 2018 AASM position statement, Baron and colleagues’ 2018 scoping review, and the 2021 AASM technology update ([Khosla et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5940440/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Baron et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6008167/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Schutte-Rodin et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.9580) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). AASM’s position is that consumer sleep technologies cannot be used to diagnose or treat sleep disorders at this time. “Better sleep” in consumer products is often a proprietary composite score; in the scholarly subset it is epoch-level agreement with PSG. Those two definitions are not the same.

The clusters talk past each other at the level of primary endpoints. The same molecule is evaluated under opposite rules in two camps: AASM 2017 suggests clinicians not use melatonin for chronic insomnia; AASM 2015 suggests strategically timed melatonin for selected circadian disorders ([Sateia et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263087/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Auger et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4582061/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Authors overlap more than endpoints do. Buysse is a hub across measurement, CBT-I, pharmacologic GRADE, and “sleep health.” This suggests that a “positive trial” in one camp can be uninterpretable in another without converting instruments.

> Conflicts noted: Melatonin is weakly recommended against for chronic insomnia and weakly recommended, when timed, for selected circadian disorders. CBT-I GRADE privileges diaries and ISI; hypnotic GRADE privileges PSG; circadian work privileges dim-light melatonin onset; public health privileges hours of sleep; consumer products privilege proprietary scores. Wan’s “most cited insomnia papers” list is contaminated by COVID psychiatry papers that are not sleep-intervention research.
>
> Gaps: No sources found for a single field-wide co-citation map that places all six axes in one network with pairwise counts. No sources found for published co-citation statistics linking DORA registration trials to CBT-I meta-analyses as a quantified “talking past” index. No sources found for wearable validation in circadian rhythm disorders (explicitly noted as absent by de Zambotti et al., 2019).

### Contested or Retracted Claims

Several widely marketed ways to improve sleep quality have been tested and then downgraded, contradicted, or never supported at trial level. Three statuses should not be collapsed. *No evidence* means the specific product was never adequately tested. *Evidence of little or no effect* means trials or guidelines found no reliable benefit. *Retracted* means a published paper was withdrawn; that is a bibliographic event, not a synonym for “the popular claim is false.”

The 2021 AASM behavioral guideline issued a conditional recommendation against sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy for chronic insomnia ([Edinger et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The 2023 European Insomnia Guideline states that sleep hygiene “on its own does not constitute an evidence-based treatment” and can even function as a safety behavior that heightens threat ([Riemann et al., 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jsr.14035) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). A 2025 meta-analysis of 42 RCTs found a pre–post ISI improvement of 3.4 points with sleep-hygiene education, but hygiene was inferior to CBT-I, partial CBT-I, and exercise, and most trials were at high risk of bias ([Chung et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40449065/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). This is evidence of inferiority as monotherapy, not “evidence of zero effect,” and not retracted.

Popular writing, including Matthew Walker’s *Why We Sleep*, has treated eight hours as a near-universal requirement. The 2015 AASM/Sleep Research Society consensus recommended 7 or more hours regularly for adults 18–60; the panel agreed that ≤6 hours was inappropriate and 7–9 hours appropriate, and could not settle 6–7 hours ([Watson et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.4716) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Alexey Guzey’s critique documented factual errors in Walker’s book; Andrew Gelman argued that an omitted-bar graph met an NIH wording of falsification in a popular book, not in a journal article ([Guzey](https://guzey.com/books/why-we-sleep/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: Medium) — **Low-to-medium independent audit**; [Gelman commentary](https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/bias/2020-gelman.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: Medium)). The book errors are not a retraction. Challenging Walker’s rhetoric does not license a claim that habitual 6-hour sleep is proven safe.

OTC melatonin is widely sold as a nightly insomnia drug. AASM’s 2017 guideline issued a weak recommendation against melatonin for adult sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia ([Sateia et al., 2017](https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.6470) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). ESRS 2023 is more split: fast-release melatonin and ramelteon are not recommended for insomnia unless circadian factors are present; prolonged-release melatonin may be considered up to 3 months in adults ≥55 ([Riemann et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38016484/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Cochrane’s 2023 review of blue-light-filtering spectacles found sleep effects indeterminate: six small RCTs, three positive and three null, very-low-certainty evidence, and no RCT data on serum melatonin ([Singh et al., Cochrane 2023](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013244.pub2/full) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

A 2026 systematic review of 12 adult magnesium RCTs concluded that current evidence does not support oral magnesium as routine insomnia treatment ([Taylor & Francis, 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19390211.2026.2719670) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Valerian is closer to a failed claim: later meta-analyses found no latency benefit and no quantitative sleep-quality benefit, and AASM 2017 issued a weak recommendation against valerian ([Fernández-San-Martín et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20347389/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Sateia et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263087/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). PolitiFact’s 16 September 2026 fact-check of “sleepy-girl” magnesium mocktails concluded that science does not conclusively show these drinks improve sleep ([PolitiFact, 2026](https://politifact.com/article/2026/sep/16/magnesium-mocktails-sleep-stress-supplement-studies/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). No sources found for an RCT of the marketed three-ingredient mocktail versus placebo.

The folk claim that sleep debt can be paid back in one long night is oversimplified. After five nights at 4 hours in bed, a single 10-hour recovery opportunity improved performance but left lapses, sleepiness, and fatigue above baseline ([Banks, Van Dongen, Dinges, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2910531/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). A 2024 systematic review of 27 alcohol studies found REM delayed and reduced even at about two standard drinks; shortened sleep-onset latency appeared only at high doses, which further worsened REM ([Gardiner et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). This is evidence of harm to sleep architecture, not merely “no evidence.”

Weighted-blanket evidence is mixed: the highest-quality pediatric RCT found no actigraphic or diary benefit ([Gringras et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25022743/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)), while adult psychiatric trials conflict on subjective versus objective outcomes. A 2025 PRISMA review of mouth taping found generally poor quality, only two studies with significant apnea or desaturation change, and explicit asphyxiation warnings when the nose is obstructed ([PLOS One 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0323643) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). AASM spokesperson John Saito called mouth taping “extremely dangerous” in a 2023 society statement ([AASM, 2023](https://aasm.org/viral-tiktok-trends-are-not-the-answer-for-better-sleep/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Grounding/earthing sheets lack adequate independent evidence of a sleep-quality effect; early papers disclose industry links ([Chevalier et al., 2012](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3265077/) (**Primary**, industry-linked; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

The claim that sleeping pills work as well as therapy long term is contradicted. Morin et al. (1999) found CBT and temazepam similar at 8 weeks, but only CBT retained gains at 12–24 months ([Morin et al., 1999](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/189099) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Sivertsen et al. (2006) found CBT superior to nightly zopiclone on PSG sleep efficiency at 6 months ([Sivertsen et al., 2006](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/203083) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Wearable marketing implies stage-level, clinical-grade tracking; later PSG comparisons show sleep-versus-wake sensitivity is typically high, but wake specificity is often 29–52%, and stage agreement is only fair to moderate ([Sensors 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11511193/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

Verified retractions in sleep medicine should not be inflated into a general collapse of the field. Robert B. Fogel’s 2003 *SLEEP* paper on obesity/apnea anatomy was retracted after an ORI finding of fabricated data ([NIH/ORI NOT-OD-09-082](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-09-082.html) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Matthew P. Walker’s 2019 *Neuron* essay “A Societal Sleep Prescription” was retracted for text duplication from a 2018 *Lancet* essay, not for data fraud ([Retraction Watch, 2020](https://retractionwatch.com/2020/07/28/high-profile-sleep-researcher-loses-paper-for-duplication/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Sharma et al.’s 2011 *NEJM* CPAP-and-metabolic-syndrome paper was retracted after primary data could not be located ([Retraction Watch, 2013](https://retractionwatch.com/2013/10/30/nejm-paper-on-sleep-apnea-retracted-when-original-data-cant-be-found/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). No sources found for a retraction of a melatonin, magnesium, valerian, blue-light-glasses, weighted-blanket, mouth-tape, grounding-sheet, or CBT-I efficacy trial that would overturn those literatures.

> Conflicts noted: AASM versus ESRS on prolonged-release melatonin in adults ≥55. NICE still offers sleep-hygiene advice first for short-term insomnia, while AASM/ESRS reject hygiene as chronic monotherapy. Weighted-blanket adult RCTs conflict with the Gringras pediatric null trial. Magnesium reviews (2021 “maybe, low certainty”) conflict with the 2026 “do not use routinely” synthesis.
>
> Gaps: No Cochrane review of sleep hygiene monotherapy; no Cochrane review of magnesium or of the sleepy-girl mocktail; no AASM/ESRS/NICE guideline on weighted blankets, mouth tape, grounding sheets, or blue-blocking glasses as insomnia therapy; no trial of wine-plus-magnesium or cherry-plus-magnesium as sold on TikTok.

### Research-to-Practice Gap

Major English-language and European guidelines now converge on the same first-line message: multicomponent CBT-I is the preferred initial treatment for chronic insomnia in adults, and sleep hygiene is not an adequate monotherapy ([Qaseem et al., 2016](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M15-2175) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Edinger et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Riemann et al., 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jsr.14035) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The 2025 VA/DoD guideline restates a strong recommendation for CBT-I and a weak recommendation against stand-alone sleep hygiene ([VA/DoD CPG 2025](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/guidelines/CD/insomnia/I-OSA-CPG_2025-Guideline_final_20250915.pdf) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

Those recommendations have been adopted on paper inside large health systems, but measured first-line delivery remains rare. One VA implementation study reported that the VHA had trained nearly 1,000 providers over about a decade, yet most patients with insomnia still receive sleeping medications instead of CBT-I ([Koffel et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7706055/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). A later electronic-record study of fiscal year 2021 found first-line CBT-I in 9,313 of 5,519,016 VA patients (0.2%) versus a new sleep-medication prescription without prior CBT-I in 225,618 (4.1%): VA patients were 24 times more likely to receive a new hypnotic than CBT-I ([Pfeiffer et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37704561/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). A Department of Defense survey cited a University of Pennsylvania directory figure of fewer than 600 registered CBT-I specialists worldwide as of 4 April 2023 ([DoD BSM resource survey](https://doi.org/10.1093/milmed/usad409) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: Medium)).

Digital CBT-I is the main adopted workaround for therapist scarcity, but coverage is geographic and contractual, not universal. In May 2022 NICE recommended Sleepio as a cost-saving primary-care option; usual NHS care is sleep-hygiene advice, and face-to-face CBT-I “is not routinely available” ([NICE HTG624](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/htg624) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). NHS Scotland pages confirm Sleepio is free to adults living in Scotland via self-referral ([NHS Highland](https://www.highlandmentalwellbeing.scot.nhs.uk/sleepio/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). In England, a UK minister stated in January 2025 that there is currently no central funding from NHS England to support national commissioning of digital CBT-I ([UK Parliament written answer](https://www.parallelparliament.co.uk/question/25006/insomnia-mental-health-services) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). In the United States, Somryst and SleepioRx have FDA clearance as computerized behavioral therapy devices; CMS created Digital Mental Health Treatment codes effective 1 January 2025, which create a reimbursement pathway, not a coverage guarantee ([FDA K233577](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf23/K233577.pdf) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [AASM news on SleepioRx](https://aasm.org/fda-clears-sleepiorx-for-chronic-insomnia/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Somryst’s current site states that availability is currently limited ([Somryst for US HCPs](https://www.somryst.com/for-us-hcps) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

Surveys of what front-line doctors actually do show a durable default of sleep hygiene plus pills. A Swiss vignette survey of 395 primary-care physicians found 87% would prescribe sleep hygiene and 65% phytopharmaceuticals for uncomplicated chronic insomnia, 8% CBT-I, 68% said they knew little about CBT-I, and 78% knew no CBT-I provider ([Maire et al.](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13169) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). In Dutch routine-care records of 1,089 first sleep-disturbance consults, GPs prescribed sleep medication to 77.0%, including 64.6% at the first visit ([BMC Prim Care](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10478592/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Japanese expert consensus ranked sleep-hygiene education first-line and multicomponent CBT-I only second-line, explicitly because of therapist shortage and lack of insurance coverage ([Takaesu et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1168100/full) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). In Sichuan, China, a survey of 420 GPs found 45.5% believed drug treatment should be first choice ([Zhou et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11475601/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Taiwan’s NHIA announced tighter management for older adults on multiple hypnotics, citing about 1.9 million hypnotic users per year and INCB 2024 data that some Taiwan BZD/Z-drug defined daily doses are about twice Korea’s and three times Australia’s ([MOHW / NHIA](https://www.mohw.gov.tw/cp-2704-86893-1.html) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Central News Agency, citing NHIA statistics, reported reimbursed hypnotic tablet volume of 435 million (2021), 461 million (2022), and 477 million (2023) ([CNA](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/ahel/202411130387.aspx) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

Consumer markets have outrun the same guidelines. A 2017 assay study found melatonin content outside a 10% label margin in more than 71% of 31 supplements, ranging from −83% to +478% of labeled dose, with serotonin in 8 of 31 products ([Erland & Saxena, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263083/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Commercial market reports put global melatonin supplements at about USD 3.79 billion in 2026; these are vendor forecasts, not audited sales ledgers ([Future Market Insights](https://www.futuremarketinsights.com/reports/melatonin-supplements-market) (**Low**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: Low)).

This suggests a practical implication for a non-specialist adult. Papers recommend a structured CBT-I course, or a brief behavioral version if that is all a clinic can deliver, with pills as a short adjunct or a fallback. What a typical adult can actually reach depends on passport and postcode. In Scotland, Sleepio is free to residents. In most of England it is not, despite NICE saying it should replace hygiene-or-pills usual care. In the United States, even VA patients are still far more likely to leave with a prescription than with CBT-I. In Japan, Taiwan, and much of China, official documents increasingly name CBT-I, but the accessible service is still a hypnotic plus sleep-hygiene leaflets. Wearables and melatonin gummies are the interventions with the lowest access barrier and the weakest claim to treat chronic insomnia.

> Conflicts noted: Japanese expert consensus ranks sleep-hygiene education first-line and multicomponent CBT-I second-line, directly opposite AASM’s recommendation against hygiene monotherapy. NICE judged Sleepio cost-saving, but NHS England has no national commissioning duty for that digital technology. VA trained more than 1,000 CBT-I clinicians yet FY2021 care remained medication-first by an order of magnitude.
>
> Gaps: No sources found for a current worldwide census of practicing CBT-I therapists comparable to Thomas et al. (2016). No sources found for a Taiwan nationwide CBT-I workforce estimate or a dedicated NHI CBT-I benefit code. No sources found for randomized trials showing that consumer wearables, used as a stand-alone intervention, produce guideline-level insomnia remission. No sources found for audited 2024–2026 global melatonin unit sales.

## State of the Field

### Open Problems

Recent adult-insomnia guidelines do not claim that first-line efficacy of CBT-I is the remaining unknown. What they name as unsolved is implementation, comparative effectiveness, durability, phenotype matching, long-term drug safety, and equity. The [AASM 2021 behavioral guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) stated there was insufficient evidence to recommend specific delivery methods and, except for CBT-I, insufficient data to evaluate efficacy within patient subgroups. The [AASM 2026 combination-treatment guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13076838/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) named as the “most important research gap” adequately powered comparative-effectiveness trials that systematically measure daytime function and comorbid outcomes, and test key subpopulations. The [ESRS 2023 European Insomnia Guideline](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jsr.14035) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) said future research must address head-to-head comparisons between all types of treatments and real-world acceptance, adherence, attrition, side-effects, and outcome. The [2021 NIH Sleep Research Plan](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/sites/default/files/publications/FINAL_NHLBI_NIH_Sleep_Research_Plan_PDF_Report_508_0.pdf) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) listed long-term comparative effectiveness of medications versus CBT-I as high-priority work.

Access is treated as an unsolved delivery problem. AASM 2026 wrote that access to CBT-I remains a problem in the general population and is an even greater challenge in underserved or rural areas, and that digital CBT-I has shown promise but engagement and dropout remain challenges ([Buysse et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13076838/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). NICE recommended Sleepio versus sleep hygiene or sleeping pills, but stated that more research is needed on Sleepio versus face-to-face CBT-I ([NICE HTG624](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/htg624) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). A 2024 equivalence meta-analysis concluded that eHealth and in-person CBT-I were statistically equivalent for most outcomes, but flagged high risk of bias and dropout ([Simonsson et al., 2024](https://mental.jmir.org/2024/1/e58217/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). An earlier secondary-care RCT had reported the opposite directional finding: face-to-face CBT-I was superior to fully automated digital CBT-I in clinic-referred patients ([Kallestad et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8664599/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

Which CBT-I components are necessary is still open. [Furukawa et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2023.5060) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) opened by saying the effect of each component remains unclear, then suggested cognitive restructuring, third-wave components, sleep restriction, and stimulus control were critical, that sleep hygiene education was not essential, and that relaxation was potentially counterproductive—while warning that undetected interactions could undermine the conclusions. [Riemann et al., 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jsr.13604) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) wrote that two out of five patients do not show full remission even after boosting CBT-I with subsequent medication, and that insight into who will respond remains limited. The subjective–objective discrepancy remains “a major clinical and scientific challenge,” and so-called misperception may in fact be mismeasurement ([Andrillon, Palagini, and Riemann, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14028) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

Long-term hypnotic and dual orexin receptor antagonist (DORA) safety is explicitly under-evidenced. ACP 2016 stated there was insufficient evidence for the balance of benefits and harms of long-term pharmacologic treatments ([Qaseem et al., 2016](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M15-2175) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). [NICE TA922](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/TA922) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) stated that daridorexant improves symptoms compared with placebo for 12 months and that effects if taken longer are unknown. A 2026 long-term DORA systematic review concluded that evidence regarding effects of six months or more remains limited ([PMC13472714](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13472714/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

Consumer wearables as interventions are not settled. The NITE RCT concluded that wearable feedback plus interpretation guidance “is likely not sufficient as a treatment for insomnia on its own” ([Spina, Bei and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10485571/) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Melatonin dose, formulation, and quality remain inconsistent; [Erland and Saxena, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263083/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High) found melatonin content from −83% to +478% of label. Exercise has some evidence, but ESRS 2023 said further high-quality studies are necessary ([Riemann et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Temperature interventions are not named as a ranked insomnia-treatment research recommendation in AASM, ACP, NICE, or ESRS; open problems come from environmental reviews that call for randomized trials of cooling ([Chevance et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.101915) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). For shift work, a 2025 review of reviews concluded that high-quality reviews found inconclusive evidence for which interventions improved sleep quality ([Cunningham et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2025.102110) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

> Conflicts noted: Digital versus in-person CBT-I is not a single finding (AASM/NICE insufficient; Simonsson 2024 equivalent; Kallestad 2021 face-to-face superior). Component necessity conflicts between AASM 2021 (protocol variation did not systematically change effectiveness) and Furukawa 2024 (specific components critical). Exercise “optima” differ across network meta-analyses and both papers treat confirmation as unfinished.
>
> Gaps: No sources found for a 2016–2026 ACP update that restates the 2016 research-needs list. No sources found for a dedicated NICE adult-insomnia clinical guideline equivalent to AASM/ESRS. No guideline-level, author-stated research agenda was found that specifies a validated short-sleep versus misperception treatment algorithm.

### Datasets and Benchmarks

The field decides whether an adult sleep-quality method “worked” with four non-interchangeable layers: validated patient-reported instruments; prospective diary parameters; objective estimates from polysomnography or research-grade actigraphy; and trial-level clinically significant thresholds that guideline groups set before GRADE review. Those layers measure different constructs. This suggests that the fairest claim that a method improved sleep quality is a pre-specified combination of a patient-reported measure (usually ISI or PSQI), diary sleep-onset latency / wake after sleep onset / sleep efficiency, and—if used—objective confirmation, judged against published responder rules rather than a single composite “sleep score.”

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index is a 19-item, 1-month retrospective questionnaire. Seven component scores sum to a global score of 0–21; a global score greater than 5 distinguished “poor” from “good” sleepers with 89.6% sensitivity and 86.5% specificity in the original sample ([Buysse et al., 1989](https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-1781(89)90047-4) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The University of Pittsburgh maintains the instrument; non-commercial research reprinting is free if the form is unaltered ([University of Pittsburgh PSQI page](https://www.sleep.pitt.edu/psqi) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The Insomnia Severity Index is a 7-item, 0–28 scale. Conventional severity bands are 0–7 none, 8–14 subthreshold, 15–21 moderate, and 22–28 severe ([Morin et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/34.5.601) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Morin et al. (2011) described a change greater than 7 as a candidate minimally important difference for moderate improvement; Yang et al. (2009) recommended a 6-point reduction ([Yang et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19689221/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The Consensus Sleep Diary is now the standardized prospective instrument for bedtime, sleep-onset latency, wake after sleep onset, total sleep time, and sleep efficiency ([Carney et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3250369/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The Epworth Sleepiness Scale estimates daytime sleep propensity, not nocturnal sleep quality ([About the ESS](https://epworthsleepinessscale.com/about-the-ess/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: Medium)). PROMIS Sleep Disturbance is an NIH item bank scored as T-scores; there is no single field-wide minimally important difference ([Buysse et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/33.6.781) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [PROMIS Sleep Scoring Manual](https://healthmeasures.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/PROMIS-Sleep-Scoring-Manual_05Dec2023.pdf) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

The 2021 AASM behavioral systematic review set between-group clinically meaningful thresholds that include diary or PSQI sleep-quality 0.5 standardized mean difference; diary/PSG sleep-onset latency 20 minutes; wake after sleep onset 20 minutes; total sleep time 15 minutes; and sleep efficiency 10 percentage points. Responder status was coded as an ISI drop of 8 or more points; remission as ISI less than 8 ([Edinger et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853211/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The 2017 AASM pharmacologic guideline used a different table (for example sleep efficiency 5 versus 10 percentage points). A method can “meet AASM 2017” and miss “AASM 2021,” or the reverse.

Large public datasets such as the Sleep Heart Health Study, MESA Sleep, and UK Biobank accelerometry can support prevalence, phenotype, and association analyses. They cannot identify whether a sleep-quality intervention worked: there is no randomized behavioral or hypnotic protocol with ISI/PSQI endpoints in those flagship cohorts ([NSRR SHHS](https://sleepdata.org/datasets/shhs) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [UK Biobank Category 1008](https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/ukb/label.cgi?id=1008) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Consumer 0–100 “sleep scores” are proprietary composites whose formulas can change with firmware. Peer-reviewed validation papers almost always test sleep/wake or stage minutes versus PSG, not the composite score ([de Zambotti et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000001947) (**Established**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Robbins et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11511193/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). This suggests that a wearable sleep score should not be a primary endpoint for deciding that a method improved sleep quality.

> Conflicts noted: ISI change thresholds are not one number (Yang 2009 MID = 6; Morin 2011 moderate improvement ≈ 8.4; AASM 2021 responder ≥8). AASM 2017 pharmacologic versus 2021 behavioral GRADE tables disagree on the size of a meaningful between-group change. Conventional sleep efficiency (total sleep time / time in bed) versus denominator-adjusted alternatives can reclassify the same diary night.
>
> Gaps: No sources found for a single official field-wide PROMIS Sleep Disturbance minimally important difference used by AASM GRADE tables. No sources found for a regulator- or AASM-endorsed definition of any consumer wearable “sleep score.” No sources found that SHHS, MESA Sleep, or UK Biobank accelerometry contain randomized adult sleep-quality interventions suitable for efficacy inference.

### Top Venues

For adult sleep-quality intervention research, prestige is split between sleep-specialty society journals, a smaller set of general medical journals that carry landmark trials and guidelines, and a short list of society congresses. *SLEEP* is the official journal of the Sleep Research Society; Oxford Academic currently displays a 2024 Journal Impact Factor of 4.9 ([SLEEP | Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/sleep) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine* is the official journal of the AASM and the primary venue for AASM clinical practice guidelines (2024 JIF 2.9 on the Springer page) ([JCSM | Springer](https://link.springer.com/journal/44470) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). *Sleep Medicine Reviews* is the leading review journal (Elsevier homepage impact factor 9.7) ([Sleep Medicine Reviews](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/sleep-medicine-reviews) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The *Journal of Sleep Research* is owned by the European Sleep Research Society; Wiley lists a 2024 JIF of 3.9 and a 15% acceptance rate ([Journal of Sleep Research | Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/journal/13652869) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). *Behavioral Sleep Medicine* is the top behavioral-science specialty title for CBT-I components and implementation (publisher-listed 2023 JIF 2.2; 16% acceptance rate) ([Behavioral Sleep Medicine](https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/hbsm20/about-this-journal) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)).

Flagship general-medicine titles remain the highest-prestige destinations when a sleep-quality RCT is large and pragmatic. Documented 2021–2026 landmarks include Kyle et al.’s 2023 *Lancet* HABIT trial of nurse-delivered sleep-restriction therapy in English primary care (n = 642; six-month ISI difference −3.05) ([Kyle et al., 2023](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(23)00683-9/fulltext) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)); Scott et al.’s 2025 *JAMA Internal Medicine* meta-analysis of CBT-I in chronic disease (67 RCTs; Hedges *g* = 0.98 for insomnia severity) ([Scott et al., 2025](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2839294) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)); and Furukawa et al.’s 2024 *JAMA Psychiatry* component network meta-analysis ([Furukawa et al., 2024](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2814164) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). No 2021–2026 adult sleep-quality intervention RCT published in flagship *JAMA* or in *Annals of Internal Medicine* was found on this search.

Society guideline issuers that set clinical standards are AASM (JCSM), ESRS (JSR), ACP (*Annals*, 2016, still current), NICE (CKS plus HTG624/Sleepio and TA922/daridorexant, not a full clinical guideline), and VA/DoD (2025 CPG). World Sleep Society endorses other societies’ guidelines after an international applicability review; it is not a primary developer ([WSS publications](https://worldsleepsociety.org/publications/) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Society scientific congresses are SLEEP (APSS annual meeting), World Sleep Congress (biennial), and ESRS Sleep Europe (biennial). World Sleep’s FAQ states typical symposium acceptance of 30–40% and typical abstract acceptance of 99% ([World Sleep FAQ](https://worldsleepcongress.com/attendees/faq) (**Primary**; accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Acceptance rates for SLEEP/APSS and ESRS Sleep Europe abstracts were not found. Industry satellite symposia at these meetings are marketing and CME adjuncts; they should not be cited as if they were AASM, ESRS, ACP, NICE, or VA/DoD recommendations.

> Conflicts noted: *SLEEP* publisher page still shows 2024 JIF 4.9 while the Australasian Sleep Association (July 2026) reported 7.0 for a later metric year ([ASA announcement](https://www.sleep.org.au/Public/News/Articles/July/Impact-factor.aspx) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: Medium)). WSS endorsed the 2017 European insomnia guideline and has not formally re-endorsed the 2023 update. NICE has no full adult-insomnia clinical guideline, which is easy to confuse with CKS and HTG624.
>
> Gaps: No published abstract acceptance rate for SLEEP/APSS or ESRS Sleep Europe. No Clarivate-login confirmation of 2025 impact factors for several specialty titles. No 2021–2026 flagship-*JAMA* or *Annals* adult sleep-quality RCT found. Current publisher-stated impact factor for *Sleep Medicine* was not verified on this access date.

## Key Findings

According to independent guideline families on both sides of the Atlantic, multicomponent cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia is the only strongly recommended first-line method for chronic adult insomnia, and it is the method with the most durable sleep-quality gains. The American College of Physicians, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, the European Sleep Research Society, NICE’s clinical knowledge summary, and the 2025 VA/DoD guideline all place CBT-I first ([Qaseem et al., 2016](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M15-2175) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Edinger et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Riemann et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [VA/DoD CPG 2025](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/guidelines/CD/insomnia/I-OSA-CPG_2025-Guideline_final_20250915.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Landmark trials and later network analyses show that CBT-I matches or exceeds hypnotics in the short term and holds gains better after treatment stops ([Morin et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.281.11.991) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Furukawa et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/pcn.13730) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The 2026 AASM combination guideline, independently confirmed on the society site and in PMC, suggests adding a hypnotic to CBT-I over a hypnotic alone, and suggests against adding a hypnotic to CBT-I over CBT-I alone ([AASM, 2026](https://aasm.org/combination-treatment-chronic-insomnia-guideline/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Buysse et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13076838/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). This suggests that for perceived sleep quality, the behavioral package is the treatment; pills are a fallback or a way to buy a little extra sleep time, not the quality engine.

The most popular “first step” in clinics and on the internet—sleep-hygiene education used alone—is the method guidelines now tell clinicians not to treat as therapy. AASM gives a conditional recommendation against hygiene as monotherapy; ESRS states that hygiene on its own does not constitute an evidence-based treatment; Furukawa’s 2024 component analysis found hygiene education non-essential (incremental odds ratio 1.01) ([Edinger et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Riemann et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Furukawa et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2023.5060) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The active ingredients that do move insomnia severity are sleep restriction, stimulus control, and cognitive restructuring—the pieces that feel least like “tips” and most like a short, uncomfortable course of changing time in bed and leaving the bedroom when awake. This suggests that a person who only receives a caffeine-and-screens leaflet has not received the treatment the papers recommend.

Consumer supplements and gadgets occupy the lowest-friction slot and the weakest evidence slot. AASM 2017 suggests clinicians not use melatonin, valerian, or diphenhydramine for chronic insomnia; ESRS 2023 does not recommend fast-release melatonin, ramelteon, antihistamines, antipsychotics, or phytotherapeutics, while allowing prolonged-release melatonin for up to three months in adults aged 55 or older ([Sateia et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263087/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Riemann et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). That transatlantic split is real and unresolved. A 2026 systematic review of 12 magnesium trials, independently confirmed on the publisher page, concluded that current evidence does not support oral magnesium as a routine insomnia treatment ([Taylor & Francis, 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19390211.2026.2719670) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Blue-blocking glasses, weighted blankets, mouth tape, grounding sheets, and “sleepy-girl” mocktails sit at insufficient, mixed, or no-evidence status, with mouth tape carrying an explicit asphyxiation warning when the nose is blocked ([Singh et al., Cochrane 2023](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013244.pub2/full) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [AASM, 2023](https://aasm.org/viral-tiktok-trends-are-not-the-answer-for-better-sleep/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). OTC melatonin products also fail basic quality control: more than 71% of assayed supplements missed a 10% label margin, and some contained serotonin ([Erland & Saxena, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263083/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). This suggests that the market is selling easy answers in the exact place guidelines have already marked “not a treatment.”

The research-to-practice gap is large enough to invert the evidence ranking in real life. Even inside the U.S. Veterans Health Administration, which trained nearly 1,000 CBT-I providers, fiscal-year 2021 patients were 24 times more likely to receive a new hypnotic than CBT-I ([Pfeiffer et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37704561/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Swiss primary-care vignettes showed 8% CBT-I versus 87% hygiene; Dutch first-visit records showed 64.6% hypnotic prescribing at the first consult; Japanese expert consensus ranked hygiene first because therapists are scarce ([Maire et al.](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13169) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Takaesu et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1168100/full) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Taiwan reimbursed hundreds of millions of hypnotic tablets a year and has begun tighter management of older long-term users ([CNA](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/ahel/202411130387.aspx) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [MOHW / NHIA](https://www.mohw.gov.tw/cp-2704-86893-1.html) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). Digital CBT-I is the main workaround, but it is free in Scotland and only locally commissioned in most of England despite a NICE cost-saving recommendation ([NICE HTG624](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/htg624) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). This suggests that access, not efficacy, is now the binding constraint on the best-supported method.

“Better sleep” is not one number, and methods that win on one instrument can lose on another. CBT-I trials treat diary continuity and the Insomnia Severity Index as critical; hypnotic trials often treat polysomnographic latency and wake after sleep onset as the decision variables; circadian papers treat dim-light melatonin onset; public-health statements treat habitual hours; consumer devices treat a proprietary 0–100 score ([Edinger et al., 2021 systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853211/) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Watson et al., 2015](https://aasm.org/resources/pdf/adultsleepdurationconsensus.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Schutte-Rodin et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.9580) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). CBT-I reliably improves perceived severity and continuity; total sleep time gains are smaller and slower ([Trauer et al., 2015](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M14-2841) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High for direction)). Caffeine and even about two drinks of alcohol degrade architecture; exercise improves subjective quality as an adjunct, not as a standalone insomnia cure ([Gardiner et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Gardiner et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High); [Riemann et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). The adult duration consensus is seven or more hours, not a rigid eight-hour rule ([Watson et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.4716) (accessed 2026-09-19, confidence: High)). This suggests that a person can “fix” a wearable score, extend time in bed, or take melatonin and still fail the outcome that guidelines treat as clinically meaningful: less complaint, less time awake after lights out, and daytime function.

## Strategic Recommendations

1. **If nights are bad three or more times a week for three months, ask for CBT-I or a validated digital CBT-I program, not a hygiene leaflet.** Rationale: this is the only method with a strong, multi-society first-line recommendation and replicated durability. Evidence: [Edinger et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/); [Riemann et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035); [Qaseem et al., 2016](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M15-2175). If a full therapist is unavailable, brief behavioral therapy or a tested digital program (Sleepio / SleepioRx / Somryst where licensed) is the next evidence-backed step, not “try melatonin first.”

2. **Treat caffeine timing, alcohol, and schedule regularity as harm-reduction, not as a complete treatment.** Rationale: recent meta-analyses show caffeine cuts total sleep time and efficiency, and even low-dose alcohol delays and shrinks REM; regularity and a fixed rise time sit inside the two-process and 3P models that still structure care. Evidence: [Gardiner et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764); [Gardiner et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030); [Borbély et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.12371). This is worth doing for almost everyone. It is not a substitute for CBT-I when insomnia is chronic.

3. **Do not rank OTC melatonin, magnesium, valerian, blue-blocking glasses, weighted blankets, mouth tape, or grounding sheets as first-line sleep-quality methods.** Rationale: guidelines recommend against or stay silent; product quality is poor; some items carry safety risk. Evidence: [Sateia et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263087/); [Riemann et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035); [Taylor & Francis, 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19390211.2026.2719670); [Erland & Saxena, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263083/). The one jurisdiction-specific exception is licensed prolonged-release melatonin for adults ≥55 in Europe/UK for a limited course—not unregulated gummies. Confidence on that exception is medium because AASM still suggests against melatonin for chronic insomnia.

4. **If medication is used, keep it short, after or beside CBT-I, and prefer a prescribed agent over an antihistamine nightcap.** Rationale: hypnotics and dual orexin antagonists have established acute efficacy and weaker long-term data; NICE places daridorexant after CBT-I failure or unavailability; OTC antihistamines are not recommended by AASM, ESRS, and NICE. Evidence: [De Crescenzo et al., 2022](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736%2822%2900878-9/fulltext); [NICE TA922](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ta922/chapter/1-Recommendations); [NICE CKS](https://cks.nice.org.uk/topics/insomnia/management/managing-insomnia/). Long-term DORA and benzodiazepine safety beyond current trial horizons remains an open problem (low-to-medium confidence for any “safest long-term pill” ranking).

5. **Health systems—especially high-hypnotic-use settings such as Taiwan—should buy access to CBT-I/BBT-I, not more hygiene content.** Rationale: the evidence ranking is already settled; the delivery ranking is inverted. Evidence: [Pfeiffer et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37704561/); [Takaesu et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1168100/full); [MOHW / NHIA](https://www.mohw.gov.tw/cp-2704-86893-1.html). Digital CBT-I plus primary-care brief behavioral therapy is the scalable pair that guidelines already endorse as substitutes when specialists are scarce.

## Risks and Uncertainties

- Data gaps: no completed Cochrane review of CBT-I for working-age adults; no Cochrane reviews of magnesium, valerian, weighted blankets, or bedroom-temperature prescriptions for primary adult insomnia; no current TSSM or Asia-Pacific GRADE insomnia-treatment guideline comparable to AASM/ESRS; no current worldwide CBT-I workforce census; no randomized trials showing that a consumer wearable, used alone, produces guideline-level insomnia remission.
- Low-confidence claims that need more validation: exact minute-level CBT-I and melatonin effect sizes from single meta-analyses; exercise network-meta rankings with unusually large point estimates; digital-versus-in-person equivalence (Simonsson 2024 versus Kallestad 2021); any numeric “optimal bedroom temperature” as insomnia therapy; commercial melatonin market-size forecasts.
- Unresolved conflicts: AASM versus ESRS/NICE on prolonged-release melatonin in adults ≥55; AASM 2015 timed melatonin for circadian disorders versus AASM 2017 against melatonin for chronic insomnia (different indications, easy to smash together); Japanese consensus ranking hygiene first versus AASM ranking it against monotherapy; AASM 2017 versus 2021 clinically meaningful thresholds that can make the same trial look positive or negative; ISI “response” cutoffs of 6 versus 7 versus 8 points.
- Domain risks: untreated sleep apnea, restless legs, or circadian disorders can look like “poor sleep quality” and will not respond to CBT-I or melatonin the same way; mouth taping can obstruct breathing; hypnotics raise falls and residual-sedation risk in older adults; unregulated supplements can be mislabeled; this report is a literature snapshot, not a diagnosis.

## Next Steps

- Recommended follow-up research: a Taiwan/East Asia access map of CBT-I and digital CBT-I (workforce, NHI codes, language-validated programs); a head-to-head living review of consumer methods against CBT-I on ISI and diary sleep efficiency; long-term DORA safety and combination designs that AASM 2026 already flagged; shift-work intervention trials that reviews currently call inconclusive.
- Decisions this research enables: what a non-specialist should try first (CBT-I / brief behavioral therapy / caffeine-alcohol-schedule, not gummies); what a clinician should not spend the first visit on (hygiene monotherapy, unregulated melatonin); what a health-system buyer should fund (validated digital CBT-I and BBT-I training).
- If the original request is not fulfilled for a specific person (shift worker, postpartum, sleep apnea, teen, older adult on many drugs), loop back and narrow the population. The present report is scoped to generally healthy adults and common chronic insomnia.
- Re-run within 30 days before acting on volatile items (NICE digital-CBT-I commissioning, FDA/CMS coverage of SleepioRx and Somryst, Taiwan NHIA hypnotic rules). Web content and reimbursement change faster than the CBT-I efficacy finding.

> ⚠️ This report summarizes publicly available research and clinical guidance as of 2026-09-19. It is not medical advice, a diagnosis, or a treatment plan. Persistent insomnia, loud snoring with breathing pauses, or sudden sleepiness while driving needs a clinician.
