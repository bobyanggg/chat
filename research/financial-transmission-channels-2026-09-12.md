## Funding and Capital Activity

### Transmission Channels

This axis maps five price-transmission channels from a weaker dollar, lost official reserve demand, or foreign official selling into U.S. equity prices. Date of research: 2026-09-12. Evidence window preferred: 2000–2026. Source types are tagged **Primary** (Fed, Treasury TIC, IMF, BIS, FOMC staff, peer-reviewed journal versions of those papers), **Established** (NBER working papers later published or widely cited academic asset-pricing papers), or **Low** (single-vendor commentary, blogs — not used for elasticities). Critical elasticities are given only when at least two independent official or academic estimates exist. Sentences that apply those elasticities to a hypothetical BRICS/SCO dump are labeled **synthesis**; stock sizes and published coefficients are labeled **fact**.

The stock of claims that could, in principle, be sold is large in dollars and small as a share of the U.S. Treasury and equity markets. The Treasury–Fed–New York Fed annual survey of foreign portfolio holdings of U.S. securities, as of 30 June 2025, measured **$35,349 billion** of foreign holdings: **$19,860 billion** in equities, **$13,840 billion** in long-term debt, and **$1,649 billion** in short-term debt. Official institutions held **$6,907 billion** of the total. Of official holdings, **$3,500 billion** were long-term Treasuries, **$405 billion** short-term debt, and **$2,230 billion** equities. Mainland China held **$1,279 billion** of U.S. securities, of which **$657 billion** were Treasuries and **$344 billion** equities. **Fact.** [U.S. Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0482) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.** Congressional Research Service, using Federal Reserve Financial Accounts table L.210 and TIC, reports foreign holdings of federal debt at about **$9.2 trillion** in December 2025, or **31 percent** of publicly held debt ($30.1 trillion), down from 34 percent in December 2021. **Fact.** [CRS / Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/RS22331) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.** Those two stock figures are not identical because the TIC annual survey is at market value and dated June, while CRS uses a broader monthly Treasury-securities concept; both agree that foreigners hold roughly one-third of publicly held Treasuries and that the official share of that foreign stock has shrunk relative to private holders.

**(1) Foreign official dump of Treasuries → higher yields / term premium → higher equity discount rates → lower PE**

**Theory (standard).** In a standard present-value identity, an unexpected rise in the discount rate that is not offset by higher expected cash-flow growth lowers the price–earnings ratio. Campbell and Shiller (1988) show that valuation ratios forecast multi-year returns more than they forecast dividend growth; the earnings–price ratio is a powerful long-horizon return predictor. **Fact (theoretical result).** [Campbell and Shiller, *Journal of Finance*](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/rsusmel/phd/campbellshiller88.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Established.** Gormsen and Lazarus (NBER 34814) decompose real-rate moves into expected growth, risk, and a “pure discounting” residual. Only the pure-discounting component transmits one-for-one into equity yields; growth and risk shocks can move bond yields and equity prices in the same direction or leave equities unmoved. They estimate U.S. market cash-flow duration of about **20 years** (they treat this as a lower bound) and find that the pure-discounting term accounts for most of the cross-country valuation change since 1990 and **77 percent** of the time variation in U.S. equity yields in their sample. **Fact.** [Gormsen and Lazarus, NBER w34814](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34814/w34814.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Established.** **Synthesis:** a term-premium shock from official selling is closer to a pure discounting / risk-premium shock than to a growth shock, so the duration arithmetic is the relevant upper bound — but only if the yield move is itself large and is not offset by a fall in the equity risk premium.

**Empirics on the first link (official flows → yields).** Two or more independent official/academic estimates exist; they do **not** agree on magnitude once horizon and identification change.

- Beltran, Kretchmer, Marquez, and Thomas (Fed IFDP 1041; *JIMF* 2013): a **$100 billion** decrease in foreign official inflows into Treasury notes and bonds in a given month raises the **5-year** yield by about **40–60 basis points** in the short run; after foreign private investors respond, the long-run effect is about **20 basis points**. Term-premium regressions in the same paper imply about **46–50 basis points** per $100 billion of official flow. A one-percent-of-outstanding change in official or private holdings lowers the term premium by **5–6 basis points** (about **17–20 basis points** per $100 billion). **Fact.** [Federal Reserve IFDP 1041](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/ifdp/2012/1041/ifdp1041.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.** [Beltran et al., *JIMF* 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261560612001647) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.**
- Warnock and Warnock (Fed IFDP 840 / NBER 12560): over January 1984–May 2005, if foreigners had not accumulated U.S. bonds over the twelve months ending May 2005, the **10-year** yield would have been **150 basis points** higher; even a step-down to average inflows of 2 percent of GDP implied **95–105 basis points**. Foreign official accumulation at its summer-2004 peak is estimated to have depressed long rates by about **90–100 basis points**. They also cite Bernanke, Reinhart, and Sack (2004): each **$1 billion** of Japanese official intervention associated with **0.7 basis points** on U.S. long rates (about **70 basis points** per $100 billion). **Fact.** [Federal Reserve IFDP 840](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/ifdp/2005/840/ifdp840.htm) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.** [Warnock and Warnock, NBER w12560](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12560/w12560.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Established.**
- Beltran et al.’s own comparison table (Treasury research-conference slides) places other studies on the same **basis-points-per-$100-billion** metric: short-run flow effects of **−66** (Bernanke et al. 2004, Japanese official), **−67** (D’Amico and King 2011, Fed daily purchases), **−70 to −100** (McCauley and Jiang 2004, weekly official flows); medium-run 12-month-flow effects of **−68** (Warnock and Warnock 2009) versus **no effect** (Rudebusch, Swanson, and Wu 2006); long-run *stock* effects of **−11 to −15** (Bertaut, DeMarco, Kamin, and Tryon 2011), **−2 to −5** (Gagnon, Raskin, Remache, and Sack 2011, Fed LSAP), **−10** (D’Amico and King), and **−4** (Hamilton and Wu 2011). **Fact (authors’ compilation of published estimates).** [Beltran et al. Treasury slides](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/276/2012-session-2-presentation-beltran.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.**
- Gagnon, Raskin, Remache, and Sack (NY Fed / *IJCB* 2011): the eight baseline LSAP announcements reduced the 10-year Treasury yield by **91 basis points** and the Kim–Wright 10-year term premium by **50–100 basis points**, with the bulk of the 10-year move attributed to term premium rather than expected short rates. Those announcements covered up to **$1.75 trillion** of assets, not $100 billion. **Fact.** [Gagnon et al., NY Fed Staff Report 441](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr441.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.**
- Kaminska, Mumtaz, and Šimović-style common-factor work (Tsinghua CICM circulating paper on foreign official demand): after controlling for foreign yields and Fed LSAPs, a **$100 billion** official sale is associated with about **60.5 basis points** on the 10-year yield and **42 basis points** on the term premium — about three times the **19 / 13.6 basis point** estimates that omit global controls. **Fact (one identification).** [CICM / Tsinghua circulating paper](http://cicm.pbcsf.tsinghua.edu.cn/__local/F/17/D2/B741CDCB746EE0D30A0A6EE4D7C_6F897A0A_CCACF.pdf?e=.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: Medium) **Established (working paper, not a Fed/IMF/BIS publication).**
- Neely (St. Louis Fed WP 2017-001): a back-of-the-envelope that starts from QE announcement effects, then *halves* them because Chinese sales cannot signal U.S. policy, *halves* them again for duration mismatch, and scales in non-U.S. bonds, concludes that sale of China’s entire then-**$3 trillion** reserve portfolio would raise U.S. yields by only about **19–32 basis points** along the curve. **Fact (author’s scenario, not an event study).** [Neely, St. Louis Fed WP 2017-001](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2017/2017-001.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.**

**The realized 2014–16 Chinese reserve-sale episode does not validate the large-flow elasticities.** China’s reserves fell on the order of **$800 billion** from June 2014; TIC-reported Chinese Treasury holdings fell about **$210 billion** (a record **$188 billion** in 2016). Over June 2014–December 2016 the 10-year yield *fell* **11 basis points** while the 5-year rose **28 basis points**. **Fact.** [U.S.–China Economic and Security Review Commission staff paper](https://www.uscc.gov/sites/default/files/Research/U.S.%20Financial%20Exposure%20to%20China.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary (U.S. government commission using TIC and yield data).** Neely’s accompanying Fed presentation notes that the August 2015 RMB move was associated with an S&P 500 drop of about **10 percent** — a China-growth / risk-off event, not a documented yield-from-dump event. **Fact.** [Neely, St. Louis Fed “Choices for China” slides](https://www.stlouisfed.org/dialogue-with-the-fed/-/media/project/frbstl/stlouisfed/files/pdfs/dwtf/choices-for-china-consequences-for-us-2016-05-10.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: Medium) **Primary.**

**Synthesis on size versus a ≥20 percent or ≥30 percent index drawdown.** Apply published elasticities to the June 2025 official long-term Treasury stock of **$3.5 trillion**, without claiming that a full dump is a political or operational baseline.

- Linear application of Beltran’s **long-run 20 bp per $100 billion** would imply **~700 basis points** for a $3.5 trillion sale. That linear extrapolation is **not** in the paper; the authors estimate monthly *flow* shocks and a cointegrating *stock* effect of 17–20 bp per $100 billion on a 1994–2010 market that was several times smaller. **Synthesis; do not treat as a forecast.**
- Linear application of Beltran’s **short-run 40–60 bp per $100 billion** to a *one-month* $100 billion official sale is what the paper actually estimates: **40–60 bp**, not hundreds. **Fact.**
- Neely’s QE-scaled scenario for a **$3 trillion** Chinese reserve liquidation is **19–32 bp**. **Fact (scenario).**
- Warnock’s **90–100 bp** figure is the estimated effect of *a year of zero official accumulation at the 2004 peak*, not a stock dump of today’s $3.5 trillion. **Fact.**
- Gormsen–Lazarus duration of **~20 years** implies that a *pure* 100 bp discount-rate shock would move the index on the order of **20 percent** (duration × Δy), and a 150 bp shock on the order of **30 percent**, *if* cash-flow expectations and the equity risk premium are held fixed. **Synthesis from their duration estimate; they explicitly warn that arbitrary rate changes do not move equities one-for-one with duration.**

**Verdict on channel 1.** The first two links (official selling → yields/term premium; discount rates → PE) are **theoretically standard** and **empirically documented**, but the documented yield elasticities span roughly **2–5 bp per $100 billion** (LSAP stock effects) to **40–60 bp per $100 billion** (Beltran short-run official flow) to **~20 bp per $100 billion** (Beltran long-run) to **19–32 bp for an entire $3 trillion Chinese reserve sale** (Neely). The 2015–16 realized sale moved yields the wrong way for a dump-crash story. **A ≥20 percent index drawdown through this channel alone is not implied by the long-run official-flow elasticities applied to any historically observed official sale; it would require either (a) treating a multi-trillion-dollar dump as a Beltran *monthly* flow shock, which the identification does not support, or (b) a 100+ bp *pure* discounting shock that is closer to Warnock’s “zero official accumulation for a full year at the 2004 peak” thought experiment than to any 2000–2026 official-sale episode. A ≥30 percent drawdown through this channel alone is not supported by the published long-run elasticities.** Confidence on the existence of *some* yield effect: **High**. Confidence that the effect is large enough, by itself, for a ≥20 percent equity crash: **Low**.

**(2) Foreign private selling of U.S. equities (TIC equity flows)**

**Theory (standard).** In an imperfectly elastic market, a negative demand shift for the market portfolio lowers prices and raises expected returns. Gabaix and Koijen’s inelastic-markets hypothesis estimates that **$1** of extra demand for equities, financed by selling bonds, raises aggregate equity-market value by about **$5** (range **$3–$8**); equivalently, a **1 percent** of market-cap inflow raises prices about **5 percent**. The implied macro demand elasticity is about **0.2**. **Fact (their IV estimate).** [Gabaix and Koijen, NBER w28967](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28967/w28967.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Established.** This is a *domestic* institutional-flow identification (Flow of Funds and 13F), not a TIC-foreign-official identification.

**Empirics on foreign flows and U.S. prices are weaker than the inelastic-markets multiplier.** Froot, O’Connell, and Seasholes (*JFE* 2001), using 1994–1998 State Street institutional trades across 46 countries, find that foreign inflows forecast subsequent emerging-market returns but **do not forecast developed-country returns**; price sensitivity to inflows is positive, and transitory inflows are followed by negative future returns. **Fact.** [Froot, O’Connell, and Seasholes, NBER w6687 / *JFE* 2001](https://www.nber.org/papers/w6687) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Established.** Brennan and Cao (1997), as summarized in later work, find that purchases of U.S. equities by four developed-country investors had **no significant impact** on U.S. market returns. Ozurk and others (*JIMF* 2010) report a positive contemporaneous association between foreign purchases of U.S. corporate stocks and the S&P 500 over 1978–2008 after controlling for rates and inflation; that is a correlation, not an identified demand shock. **Fact.** [Ozurk et al., *JIMF* 2010 abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1042443109000377) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: Medium) **Established.** Fed staff (Bertaut and Judson) warn that TIC *transactions* and TIC *holdings* for equities have long been hard to reconcile; for February–June 2023, a valuation-adjusted holdings method implied about **$175 billion** of foreign net *sales* of U.S. equity while the new SLT-reported transactions showed **$133 billion** of net *purchases*. **Fact.** [Federal Reserve FEDS Note, 2 Oct 2023](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/measuring-u-s-cross-border-securities-flows-new-data-and-a-guide-for-researchers-20231002.html) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.** Ülkü (*International Review of Finance*) finds that TIC-based foreign-flow studies understate persistence and contemporaneous return correlation relative to destination-compiled data. **Fact.** [Ülkü, *International Review of Finance*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/irfi.12051) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: Medium) **Established.**

**Stock that could be sold.** Official equity holdings were **$2,230 billion** on 30 June 2025 versus **$19,860 billion** of *all* foreign equity holdings — official institutions are a Treasury-heavy, equity-light holder. **Fact.** [U.S. Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0482) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.** A vendor series puts S&P 500 capitalization at **$52.50 trillion** on 30 June 2025 (the TIC survey date) and **$58.44 trillion** at year-end 2025. **Fact (vendor).** [YCharts S&P 500 market cap](https://ycharts.com/indicators/sp_500_market_cap) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: Medium) **Established (data vendor, not Fed/IMF/BIS).**

**Synthesis on size.** Applying Gabaix–Koijen’s multiplier of 5 to a *complete* official equity liquidation of $2.23 trillion against a $52.5 trillion S&P 500 would be a **4.2 percent** of market-cap flow and a **~21 percent** price move if (i) the multiplier estimated on domestic 13F/FoF flows applies one-for-one to foreign official sales, (ii) counterparties do not absorb the flow at a higher elasticity, and (iii) there is no offsetting private foreign buying. None of those three conditions is documented for official or BRICS equity sales. **Synthesis; confidence Low on the 21 percent figure.** A 1 percent of S&P flow ($525 billion) would be about **5 percent** on the same multiplier — well below a 20 percent drawdown. Reaching **20 percent** under M = 5 requires about **$2.1 trillion** of *net* foreign selling (~11 percent of the $17.6 trillion private foreign equity stock); reaching **30 percent** requires about **$3.2 trillion**. No TIC episode in 2000–2026 is documented in Fed or IMF sources as a $2 trillion *transaction* (as opposed to valuation) sale of U.S. equities. The 2023 TIC measurement discrepancy of roughly **$300 billion** between two official methods in a five-month window is itself a reminder that inferred “sales” can be valuation error. **Synthesis.**

**Verdict on channel 2.** Theoretically standard under inelastic demand; empirically documented as a *contemporaneous correlation* for U.S. foreign equity purchases, **not** as a stable TIC-to-S&P elasticity, and **not** as a predictor of developed-market returns in Froot et al. Officials do not hold enough U.S. equity for a reserve-diversification dump to be the main object. **A ≥20 percent drawdown through official equity selling alone is not supported. A ≥20 percent drawdown through private foreign selling would require a multi-trillion-dollar net TIC sale that is not in the 2000–2026 official record; it is a tail demand-shift, not a measured BRICS policy channel. A ≥30 percent drawdown is still one multiplier application further from the data.** Confidence that *some* foreign equity flow moves U.S. prices: **Medium**. Confidence that observed or plausible official selling produces ≥20 percent: **Low**.

**(3) USD depreciation → S&P earnings translation versus imported inflation versus Fed tightening**

Three sub-channels have opposite signs.

**3a. Translation (weaker dollar → higher reported foreign earnings).** S&P Dow Jones Indices’ own foreign-sales reports put S&P 500 foreign sales at **43.6 percent** of sales in 2017 and **42.9 percent** in 2018 (recent high **47.8 percent** in 2014). **Fact.** [S&P DJI via PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/sp-500-foreign-sales-for-2017-total-43-6-300698039.html) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary (index provider).** [S&P DJI, “S&P 500 2018: Global Sales”](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/research/sp-500-global-sales-2018.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.** FactSet’s earnings-season commentary used a **40 percent** international-revenue figure for the S&P 500 when discussing 2022 dollar strength. **Fact.** [FactSet](https://insight.factset.com/50-of-sp-500-companies-citing-a-negative-impact-from-fx-on-q3-earnings-calls-to-date) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: Medium) **Established (data vendor).** **Synthesis, not a published elasticity:** if 40–43 percent of sales are foreign-currency and unhedged, a 10 percent dollar depreciation raises *reported* sales on the order of **4 percent**; hedges, foreign-currency costs, and the fact that some “foreign sales” are dollar-invoiced all cut that number. No Fed, IMF, or BIS paper was found that converts a 10 percent dollar move into an S&P 500 *index-level* earnings or price elasticity sufficient for a 20 percent drawdown. Directionally this sub-channel is an **equity tailwind**, not a crash channel.

**3b. Imported inflation (weaker dollar → higher import and consumer prices).** Gopinath (*The International Price System*, NBER 21646) estimates that a **10 percent** dollar depreciation raises U.S. dollar import prices by **3.4 percent** after one quarter and **4.4 percent** after eight quarters (long-run pass-through about **44 percent**), versus near-complete pass-through in local-currency-invoicing economies. She estimates the direct CPI effect of a 10 percent dollar depreciation at **0.4–0.7 percentage points** over two years. **Fact.** [NBER Digest summary of Gopinath w21646](https://www.nber.org/digest/jan16/cross-country-differences-exchange-rate-effects-inflation) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Established.** Gopinath, Itskhoki, and Rigobon (*AER* 2010) show that, even conditional on a price change, pass-through is about **25 percent** for dollar-priced U.S. imports versus **95 percent** for non-dollar-priced imports. **Fact.** [Gopinath, Itskhoki, and Rigobon, *AER* 2010](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.100.1.304) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Established.** Boston Fed staff (Cavallo, Neiman, and related 2015 current-policy paper) estimate short-run pass-through into U.S. consumer prices of about **1.6–1.9 percent** and compute that a **15 percent** dollar *appreciation* lowers consumer prices about **0.24 percent** in the short run and **0.4 percent** after two years (the depreciation mirror is the same order). **Fact.** [Boston Fed](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/economic/cpp1509.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.** Mishkin (Fed Governor speech, 2008) notes that estimated pass-through into U.S. consumer prices has declined over two decades and that a large dollar decline need not imply a large inflation impulse if expectations stay anchored. **Fact.** [Mishkin, Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/mishkin20080307a.htm) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.**

**3c. Fed reaction.** No FOMC publication was found that maps “X percent dollar depreciation → Y basis points of the funds-rate path” as a rule. The 2022 tightening cycle that *did* produce a ≥20 percent equity drawdown was a response to **domestic** inflation near multi-decade highs, and it was accompanied by a *stronger* dollar, not a weaker one. **Fact (sign of the dollar in 2022); the 2022 bear market is established market history.** Using bank research for the exact 27.5 percent / 284 bp pairing would be **Low** relative to the user’s source bar and is not used as an elasticity here.

**Verdict on channel 3.** Translation is **theoretically standard** and **empirically visible** in firm-level FX commentary; its sign for S&P *earnings* is **positive** when the dollar falls. Pass-through into U.S. CPI is **theoretically standard** and **empirically documented** as **small** (roughly **0.4–0.7 percentage points** of CPI per 10 percent dollar decline over two years). That is not, on published coefficients, a 2022-sized inflation shock. **A ≥20 percent or ≥30 percent index drawdown from dollar depreciation via inflation-plus-Fed-tightening is not supported by the pass-through literature; the earnings-translation sub-channel works against a crash.** Confidence on small U.S. CPI pass-through: **High**. Confidence that a BRICS-driven dollar decline would force crash-sized tightening: **Low**.

**(4) Loss of “exorbitant privilege” cheap funding for U.S. corporates**

**Theory (standard, but the object is the U.S. *external* balance sheet, not the S&P 500’s WACC).** Gourinchas and Rey (2007) measure privilege as the excess real return on U.S. external assets over external liabilities: **2.11 percent** per year over 1952–2004 and **3.32 percent** over 1973–2004, of which **384 basis points** on debt and **340 basis points** on equity, with almost no excess return on FDI. **Fact.** [Gourinchas and Rey, NBER w11563](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11563/w11563.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Established / Primary (NBER/G7 volume).** The counterpart is an “exorbitant duty”: in 2007:3–2009:1 the U.S. net foreign asset trajectory implied a transfer on the order of **19 percent of GDP** to the rest of the world as the dollar rose and U.S. risky assets fell. **Fact.** [Gourinchas, Rey, and Govillot, IMES / Bank of Japan](https://www.imes.boj.or.jp/research/papers/english/10-E-20.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.** IMF/Fed staff using confidential security-level TIC (Bertaut, Curcuru, Faia, and Gourinchas, IMF WP 2024/241 and Fed IFDP 1398) revise the *portfolio* excess return down to **0.5 percent** per year over 2005–2022 and **1.7 percent** excluding 2020–22; it is **−3.1 percent** in identified global-crisis windows and **+3.3 percent** in non-crisis windows. Composition (U.S. claims tilted to equity) — not a uniform cheap-liability coupon — does most of the work. **Fact.** [IMF WP 2024/241](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2024/english/wpiea2024241-print-pdf.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.**

**Convenience yield on *Treasuries* (the piece that could raise corporate discount rates).** Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jørgensen (NBER 12881 / *JPE* line of work) estimate a downward-sloping demand for Treasury convenience: raising Debt/GDP from **0.38 to 0.39** narrows the AAA–Treasury spread by about **1.5–4.25 basis points**; at then-prevailing debt levels Treasuries yielded on the order of **70 basis points** less than they would without convenience. The annual value of that liquidity is estimated at **0.21–0.54 percent of GDP**. **Fact.** [Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jørgensen, NBER w12881](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12881/w12881.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Established.** [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/aug07/treasury-debt-and-corporate-bond-rates) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Established.** Jiang, Krishnamurthy, and Lustig (*Journal of Finance*; NBER 24439) measure the *foreign* convenience yield from the Treasury basis (U.S. Treasury yield minus the currency-hedged foreign-government yield). A one-standard-deviation convenience-yield shock widens the annualized basis by about **20 basis points** and appreciates the dollar about **3 percent** over two quarters; they attribute on the order of **90 percent** of the Treasury convenience yield to the dollar denomination rather than Treasury-specific liquidity, and discuss an additional convenience yield for foreigners on the order of **2 percent** per year in their calibration. **Fact.** [Jiang, Krishnamurthy, and Lustig, NBER w24439](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24439/w24439.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Established.**

**What this is not.** The NY Fed “exorbitant privilege” paper on QE and BBB “fallen angels” (staff report 1004) is about a *domestic* rating-clientele subsidy under QE, not about reserve-currency status. It is not used here as a BRICS elasticity.

**Synthesis on corporate funding and equities.** Loss of privilege, if it means a vanishing Treasury convenience yield of **70–200 basis points**, raises *Treasury* yields toward the yield on other safe bonds. Whether U.S. *corporate* yields rise one-for-one, or by less because the AAA–Treasury *spread* compresses as convenience disappears (the KVJ comparative-statics), is not settled in the papers above. Even a **100 basis point** rise in corporate discount rates is the same duration arithmetic as channel 1: enough *in theory* for a ~20 percent index move if it is a pure discounting shock and cash-flow growth is unchanged. No paper in this set estimates that BRICS reserve diversification has removed, or will remove, 100 basis points of U.S. corporate WACC. The IMF 2024 revision already shrinks the measured privilege in the TIC era, which **cuts** the remaining privilege that a future dump could destroy. **Synthesis.**

**Verdict on channel 4.** Theoretically standard as an *external-account* and *safe-asset* phenomenon; empirically documented for Treasury convenience and for the U.S. external excess return; **not** empirically documented as a large, separate U.S. *equity* crash channel. **A ≥20 percent drawdown would require treating the entire convenience yield as a pure equity discount-rate shock that disappears in one step — that step is not in the 2000–2026 data. A ≥30 percent drawdown is one further unmeasured increment.** Confidence that privilege exists and is smaller than the 1973–2004 Gourinchas–Rey headline: **High**. Confidence that losing it produces a ≥20 percent S&P crash: **Low**.

**(5) Risk-off flight *into* U.S. assets (the opposite channel)**

**Theory (standard).** The same literature that gives the United States a privilege in normal times gives it an insurance role in crises: global risk-off raises demand for dollar safe assets, appreciates the dollar, and *lowers* Treasury yields. Miranda-Agrippino and Rey’s global financial cycle is the complementary risky-asset factor. Kekre and Lenel (NBER 29238) and Fed IFDP 1381 (Bergant, Coppola, Di Giovanni, and Kalemli-Özcan / related “Global Flight to Safety” work) formalize dollar-biased flight-to-safety shocks that raise the convenience of dollar bonds relative to foreign bonds and *reduce* global output. **Fact (model plus estimated shock).** [Kekre and Lenel, NBER w29238](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29238/revisions/w29238.rev1.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Established.** [Federal Reserve IFDP 1381](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/ifdp/files/ifdp1381.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.**

**Empirics.** McCauley and McGuire (BIS *Quarterly Review*, December 2009) document that in the second half of 2008 the dollar *appreciated* because of a flight into Treasury bills, carry-trade reversal, dollar-funding shortage, and overhedging — even as private foreigners sold U.S. corporate and agency bonds. **Fact.** [McCauley and McGuire, BIS Quarterly Review, Dec 2009](https://www.bis.org/publications/dollar-appreciation-2008-safe-haven-carry-trades-dollar-shortage-and-overhedging_1.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.** That episode is also the largest U.S. equity crash in the sample: equities fell while the dollar and T-bills were bid. Flight-to-safety is therefore **not an equity-price support** in levels; it is a **bond-and-dollar support** that can coincide with a ≥30 percent equity crash whose *cause* is global risk-off, not a reserve dump. Gourinchas and Rey’s duty estimate (≈19 percent of GDP wealth transfer in 2007–09) is the stock-market-and-dollar valuation of that insurance. **Fact.** [Gourinchas, Rey, and Govillot](https://www.imes.boj.or.jp/research/papers/english/10-E-20.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.**

BIS (December 2020 box) finds that after about 2000, 10-year yields typically *fell* when the S&P 500 dropped 1 percent on sell-off days (Treasuries hedged equities), but that hedge weakened after 2018. **Fact.** [BIS Quarterly Review, Dec 2020](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2012w.htm) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.** BIS Bulletin 105 documents April 2025 as an unusual episode in which U.S. equities, Treasuries, and the dollar fell together; the authors argue the dollar slide is more consistent with **ex-post FX hedging** of unhedged dollar assets than with a structural loss of reserve demand, noting that Treasury prices *rose* during Asian hours while the dollar fell. **Fact (authors’ interpretation of high-frequency data).** [BIS Bulletin 105](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull105.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-12, confidence: High) **Primary.**

**Verdict on channel 5.** Theoretically standard and **empirically documented** in 2008 and in estimated GFS shocks. Its sign for the *dollar and Treasuries* is the opposite of channels 1 and 3’s “dump and depreciate” narrative. Its sign for *equities* is typically negative in a risk-off, because the global risk factor hits equities even as it bids the dollar. **This channel can produce a ≥20 percent or ≥30 percent U.S. equity drawdown — 2008 is the existence proof — but the cause in that episode was a financial-system crash, not BRICS reserve diversification. Using it as a de-dollarization crash channel reverses the mechanism the papers estimate: a challenge that *raises global risk* while the dollar remains the safe asset supports Treasuries and the dollar and still allows equities to crash for risk-off reasons; a challenge that *destroys* dollar safety would disable this buffer (the April 2025 co-sale is the closest documented hint, and BIS does not attribute it to BRICS/SCO).** Confidence that risk-off bids the dollar and Treasuries: **High**. Confidence that BRICS/SCO policy is the 2008-sized risk-off: **Low**.

---

**Cross-channel synthesis (not a new estimate).** Channels 1 and 4 are the only ones in which a *measured* official-reserve object (Treasuries, convenience yield) maps, in published papers, into U.S. discount rates. Their **documented** yield effects for historically observed official sales are tens of basis points, not the 100–150 bp pure-discounting shock that duration arithmetic needs for a 20–30 percent PE compression. Channel 2 is large enough on paper only if one applies a domestic inelastic-markets multiplier to a multi-trillion-dollar *private* equity sale that TIC has not recorded as a transactions shock. Channel 3’s inflation arithmetic is too small to force 2022-style tightening, and its earnings arithmetic has the wrong sign for a crash. Channel 5 is the historically crash-capable channel, and it runs **into** dollar assets. **Fact versus synthesis boundary:** the elasticities and stocks above are facts from the cited papers; the statement that they do not add up to a BRICS/SCO-caused ≥20 percent S&P crash *without* an additional, undocumented demand shock is **synthesis**.

> Conflicts noted:
> - Official-flow yield elasticities disagree by an order of magnitude once horizon is changed: Beltran short-run **40–60 bp / $100bn**, Beltran long-run **~20 bp / $100bn**, Warnock 12-month “zero official flow” **~90–100 bp** (not per $100bn), Bertaut et al. stock **11–15 bp / $100bn**, Gagnon/Hamilton–Wu LSAP stock **2–10 bp / $100bn**, common-factor official-sale estimate **~60 bp / $100bn** on the 10-year, Neely full-China-reserve scenario **19–32 bp for $3 trillion**. Linear scaling of the short-run coefficient to a multi-trillion dump is inconsistent with Neely and with the 2015–16 episode.
> - Rudebusch, Swanson, and Wu (2006), as tabulated by Beltran, find **no** medium-run official-flow effect, against Warnock’s **−68 bp / $100bn**.
> - 2015–16 Chinese Treasury holdings fell ~$210 billion while the 10-year yield **fell** 11 bp — opposite the dump-raises-yields prediction (confounded by a China/commodity disinflation shock).
> - TIC equity “sales” can have the opposite sign of SLT-reported transactions in the same months (Fed FEDS Note, 2023).
> - Gourinchas–Rey privilege of **2–3.3 percent** (1952–2004 / 1973–2004) versus IMF/Fed 2024 **0.5 percent** (2005–2022) on the portfolio book.
> - April 2025 (dollar, bonds, and equities down together) versus the 2008 BIS pattern (dollar and T-bills up, equities down). BIS attributes 2025 to hedging, not to a tested loss of reserve status.
> - Equity duration (~20 years) is **not** the elasticity of the S&P to an arbitrary yield change (Gormsen–Lazarus); applying duration to a Beltran yield shock overstates the equity move if the shock is not “pure discounting.”
>
> Gaps:
> - No Fed, IMF, or BIS paper estimates a BRICS/SCO-specific Treasury-sale path or an official “dump” as an identified shock after 2016.
> - No published TIC-equity-flow elasticity for the S&P 500 in units of “percent index per $100 billion,” comparable to the Beltran yield elasticity. Applying Gabaix–Koijen to TIC is an out-of-sample synthesis.
> - No official mapping from dollar depreciation to the federal-funds path (the missing link in channel 3c).
> - No paper isolates the U.S. corporate WACC subsidy that is *specifically* due to foreign official Treasury demand, as opposed to the Treasury convenience yield or the external excess return.
> - Official versus private foreign Treasury holdings at market value are not identical across TIC annual survey, monthly TIC, and Financial Accounts; China attribution in TIC remains a known custody-bias problem (Warnock; Fed notes).
> - S&P DJI has not (in sources located on 2026-09-12) published an index-wide foreign-sales share for 2024–2025 comparable to the 2016–2018 reports; 40 percent is a FactSet working number.
> - No paper in this set tests whether a ≥20 percent or ≥30 percent S&P drawdown is the *conditional* effect of a reserve-currency shock after controlling for U.S. recession, Fed tightening, and global risk-off.
