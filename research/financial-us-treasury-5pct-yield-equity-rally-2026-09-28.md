## Price Facts — Treasury Yields

The 5% break in late September 2026 is a **U.S. Treasury** story, not a Taiwan or China government-bond story, and it is concentrated in the **intermediate and long end**, not the cash or two-year rate. According to the U.S. Treasury’s daily par yield curve (constant-maturity, or CMT, closes built from about 3:30 p.m. bid-side quotes), the 10-year first **closed at 5.00% on 2026-09-15**, after starting September at 4.79% on 2026-09-01; it then printed 5.01% on 2026-09-16, slipped back to 4.94% on 2026-09-17 and 4.96% on 2026-09-21 and 2026-09-22, and **reclaimed 5% on a closing basis on 2026-09-23 at 5.11%**, then 5.18% on 2026-09-24 and **5.17% on 2026-09-25**, the latest official close as of this writing. ## Drivers of the 5% Yield Break

The late-September 2026 break of 5% on the U.S. 10-year was not a single-print event. According to [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/10/investing/oil-iran-war-diesel) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Established**, the 10-year surged more than 10 basis points to 4.97% on 10 September as Brent crude settled at $107.63 a barrel. A later CNN recap said the 10-year had already topped 5% earlier in September before pulling back and then jumping about 15 basis points on 23 September to 5.11%. [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/us-30-year-bond-yield-rises-highest-since-2004-selloff-deepens-2026-09-24/) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Established** put the 10-year at 5.20% and the 30-year at 5.48% on 24 September. This suggests the 5% break was a mid-September breach that then accelerated through 23–25 September.

Established outlets describe a stacked set of drivers: sticky inflation, a still-solid labor market, a mid-cycle Fed hike plus another hike in the dots, large fiscal deficits as backdrop, a weak 5-year auction, a Treasury buyback program that did not cap long yields, a firmer dollar, and an oil shock from the Iran war. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/25/treasury-yields-bonds-debt.html) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Established** attributed the late-week surge to hawkish comments from Fed Governor Michael Barr, stubbornly high oil prices, and an S&P Global PMI at a more-than-four-year high. Reuters quoted TD Securities’ Gennadiy Goldberg saying “the vast majority of the move higher in yields since March has been driven by rising Fed expectations, with the remainder driven by a combination of rising growth expectations and higher oil prices.” [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/26/10-year-treasury-yield-is-at-its-highest-in-19-years-how-we-got-here.html) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Established** separately quoted Macquarie’s Thierry Wizman arguing that “this year it has more to do with the bond issuance than the inflation story.” Those two Established readings conflict on the relative weight of expected short rates versus supply; they are not mutually exclusive.

On inflation, the last full CPI print before the late-week lurch was August. According to the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_09112026.htm) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary**, CPI-U rose 0.4% seasonally adjusted in August after 0.1% in July, and 3.4% over the 12 months ending August. Gasoline rose 3.9% in August and accounted for more than one-third of the monthly all-items increase; core CPI rose 0.3% on the month and 2.4% over the year. [BEA](https://www.bea.gov/news/2026/personal-income-and-outlays-july-2026) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary** last reported July PCE up 0.2% on the month and 3.7% over the year, with core PCE up 0.2% and 3.3%; August PCE was scheduled for 30 September. The [September 2026 SEP](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcprojtabl20260916.htm) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary** raised the median 2026 PCE inflation projection to 3.7% from 3.6% in June, with a return to 2% not until 2029.

The labor market print immediately before the September FOMC was stronger than the prior year’s run-rate. According to [BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary**, nonfarm payrolls rose 162,000 in August and the unemployment rate was unchanged at 4.1%. On 16 September the FOMC voted 12–0 to raise the funds-rate target by 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00%. [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary**. The SEP median funds-rate path was 4.1% at end-2026 and 4.1% in 2027. The 2026 dots show 12 participants at 4.125 (one more hike after September) and four at 4.375 (two more). On 23 September, Governor Michael Barr said “in my base case, further policy adjustments are likely to be needed.” [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/files/barr20260923a.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary**. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/23/market-sees-next-fed-hike-in-october-following-barr-comments-hot-inflation.html) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Established** said CME FedWatch October hike odds jumped to 73% that day.

Fiscal facts are large but not a debt-ceiling crisis in this window. The official [Monthly Treasury Statement for August 2026](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/static-data/published-reports/mts/MonthlyTreasuryStatement_202608.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary** shows a fiscal-year-to-date deficit of $1.966 trillion. The [Daily Treasury Statement for 24 September](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/static-data/published-reports/dts/DailyTreasuryStatement_20260924.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary** showed TGA closing at $924.627 billion, total public debt outstanding $40.069 trillion, and about $1.22 trillion of debt-ceiling headroom. ## Historical Analogues

The last time the 10-year printed near 5%, in October 2023, U.S. equities sold off first and then rallied hard once yields fell. FRED’s daily close peaked at 4.98% on 19 October 2023 and never closed at or above 5% that month. [FRED DGS10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS10) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary**. S&P Dow Jones Indices reported that the note nonetheless traded as high as 5.02%. [S&P DJI](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/commentary/market-attributes-us-equities-202310.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary**. Reuters said the S&P 500 had “slid over 8% since late July” as the 10-year climbed about a full percentage point. [Reuters, 26 Oct 2023](https://www.reuters.com/markets/rates-bonds/soaring-treasury-yields-threaten-long-term-performance-us-stocks-2023-10-26/) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Established**. After yields broke, November 2023 delivered a large bounce. This suggests the 2023 5% print was a brief valuation shock that reversed when the Fed-pivot narrative arrived, not a regime that equities had to live with. **2026 is different on that point: yields stayed above 5% and the Nasdaq still made a record.**

The last *sustained* period with the 10-year above 5% was 2006–07. FRED first closed at 5.05% on 13 April 2006, peaked at 5.26% on 12 June 2007, and last closed at or above 5% on 19 July 2007. [FRED DGS10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS10) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary**. Compiled S&P 500 tables put 2006 at about +15.8% and 2007 at about +5.5% total return. The equity peak came later (9 October 2007), then a 56.8% peak-to-trough decline into March 2009. By the time that drawdown was underway, the 10-year was already well below 5%. This suggests a 5% 10-year can sit inside a still-rising bull market for many months; the GFC analogue is a lag and a different shock, not “5 percent, therefore crash.”

2022 is the anti-analogue. The 10-year rose from 1.63% on 3 January 2022 to 4.25% on 24 October; the S&P 500 fell about 25% peak-to-trough that year. [FRED DGS10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS10) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary**; [FRED SP500](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/SP500) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High) **Primary**. ## Key Findings

The 5% break is a **U.S. 10-year and long-bond** event, not a cash-rate event and not a Taiwan or China government-bond event. According to the U.S. Treasury par curve, the 10-year first closed at 5.00% on 15 September 2026, pulled back below 5%, then closed at 5.11% on 23 September and 5.17% on 25 September; on-the-run intra-day prints reached about 5.23%. [U.S. Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?field_tdr_date_value=2026&type=daily_treasury_yield_curve) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High). The 30-year had already closed above 5% every session since 7 July. The 2-year never closed at 5% in September (month-high close 4.87%). Overnight funds and SOFR sat near 3.88% after the 16 September hike to a 3.75–4.00% target. China 10-year CGBs were about 1.67% and Taiwan’s 10-year about 1.96%. This suggests popular “國債破5%” shorthand is describing **long-term U.S. discount rates**, not the policy rate and not local Asian bonds.

“Stocks kept rising” is true for **cap-weighted, AI-led indexes** and false for the average stock. The S&P 500 closed 25 September at 7,743.41, up 1.2% on the week and 13.1% year-to-date, but still about 0.7% below its 13 August record of 7,798.99. [Associated Press via WTOP](https://wtop.com/national/2026/09/how-major-us-stock-indexes-fared-friday-9-25-2026/) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High). The Nasdaq Composite made a record close of 27,244.28 on 22 September and finished the week up 2.1%. The Russell 2000 fell 0.8% that week; the Dow was down about 2.6% month-to-date; Reuters said eight of 11 S&P sectors were negative in September and equal-weight was down about 4%. VIX closed 25 September at 14.87. This pattern indicates the apparent stocks-versus-bonds puzzle is largely an **index-composition** story: a handful of mega-cap and semiconductor names carried the headline indexes while rate-sensitive groups (utilities, REITs, homebuilders, many financials) already absorbed the 5% yield.

The last leg of the yield spike is best documented as a **growth-plus-Fed-plus-oil** stack, with fiscal supply as the multi-month backdrop rather than a September coupon flood. Primary data show August CPI at 3.4% year-over-year with a 0.4% monthly pop driven by gasoline, a 16 September 25-basis-point hike, SEP dots implying another 2026 hike, a 23 September flash composite PMI of 58.4, and Barr’s “further policy adjustments” speech that same day. [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High); [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/us-30-year-bond-yield-rises-highest-since-2004-selloff-deepens-2026-09-24/) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High). Coupon auction sizes were held at the August refunding; September CMBs were $0 through the 24th; long-end buybacks printed **below** their new caps ($5.2 billion, then $4.1 billion) and still did not stop the selloff. San Francisco Fed Christensen–Rudebusch data on 24 September assign most of the 12-month 10-year rise to expected short rates, not term premium. [SF Fed](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/data-and-indicators/treasury-yield-premiums/) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High). ACM and Kim–Wright disagree on even the sign of the year-to-date term-premium change, so a clean “bond vigilante” claim for *this week* is under-identified.

Equities could live with that yield rise because **the earnings numerator rose faster than the multiple fell**, and because many commentators read the yield rise as “good” (strong real growth and AI investment) rather than “bad” (fiscal panic). FactSet’s 18 September note had calendar-2026 S&P earnings growth at 31.8% and a forward P/E of 19.1, down from 20.4 on 30 June, while forward EPS was up 8.8% over that window versus a 1.8% price gain. [FactSet](https://advantage.factset.com/hubfs/Website/Resources%20Section/Research%20Desk/Earnings%20Insight/EarningsInsight_091826.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-28, confidence: High). BlackRock and Goldman’s Kostin both stated the same rule: what drives yields matters. Cross-asset prints fit that reading more than a crisis reading: the dollar rose, gold fell on the week, IG OAS barely moved, and VIX stayed in the mid-teens. The Fed-model gap versus a *nominal* 5% 10-year is roughly zero on a 19x multiple; Goldman’s still-comfortable 270 bp ERP is versus the *real* 10-year. This suggests the split is an earnings-and-narrative coexistence, not proof that 5% has stopped mattering.

History says 5% is a watch zone, not a crash switch, but 2026 does not map onto 2023. In October 2023 the 10-year only *traded* 5% intra-day, the S&P dropped about 8–10% from late-July highs, and stocks then rallied hard as yields fell. In 2006–07 the S&P kept rising for more than a year after 5% prints; the crash came later from credit, after yields had already left 5%. 2022 remains the anti-analogue (yields up from 1.6%, CPI at 9.1%, stocks and bonds down together). Named 2026 breakpoints conflict: Sharma’s 5% AI-bubble tripwire has already been printed without an index crash; Barclays still calls 5% an inflection while staying constructive; J.P. Morgan conference chatter moved the fear line to 5.5–6.0%; Woodard’s summer line was “5 is the warning, 7 is the one that hurts.” The pattern across sources indicates the live risk is **speed plus an earnings miss**, not the first tick through 5.00.

## Strategic Recommendations

1. **Treat “stocks up / yields up” as a mega-cap statement, not a market statement.** The cap-weighted S&P and Nasdaq can print green weeks while equal-weight, Russell 2000, utilities, REITs, and homebuilders are already in a rate-shock drawdown. Evidence: AP weeklies through 25 September; CNBC 23 September breadth piece; Sterling sector week. If the question is “is risk appetite intact?”, RSP, IWM, and 52-week-low counts are the cleaner gauges.

2. **Use Q3 2026 earnings, not the 5.00 handle, as the falsification test.** FactSet’s 28.9% Q3 growth estimate has already been revised *up* intra-quarter. Barclays, SSGA, Cetera, and BMO all say the tape has little room for an AI or broad-EPS miss. If that bar is cleared, house base cases (BlackRock OW, Barclays “reluctantly optimistic,” Goldman 12-month ~8,700) remain internally consistent. If it is missed, the 5% yield stops being offset and starts behaving like Barclays’ “persistent headwind.”

3. **Re-anchor the danger zone from 5.0% to a rapid move through 5.5–6.0%, and to time spent above 5%.** The 5.00 print has already lost shock value in the data (Nasdaq record on 22 September). Reuters’ JPM conference recap is the best Established source for the new threshold; Sharma’s remaining claim is *duration* above 5%, which is still open. GSAM’s speed rule (~40 bp in a month) is more useful than a round-number level.

4. **Do not overweight Treasury buybacks or a September coupon flood as the reason yields broke 5%.** Official results show operations undershooting new caps; coupon sizes were unchanged; CMBs were $0 through 24 September. Treat buybacks as a failed *cap* (they were never officially a cap) and as a rounding error versus a $32 trillion privately held market. Fiscal deficits remain the stock-and-trajectory risk for 2027 coupon increases (TBAC’s $1.45 trillion shortfall), not the 23–25 September catalyst.

5. **Treat Hormuz diplomacy as two-way for *both* legs of the split.** 22 September (reopening talk, oil down, stocks up, yields easing) and 24 September (talks stalling, oil back above $105, new yield highs) already demonstrated the fork. A real reopening would be equity-friendly and would likely *end* the yields-up half of the split. A re-escalation is the Wilson-style path toward a 7,100-ish S&P dip. This is a geopolitical overlay on a growth/Fed story, not the whole story.
