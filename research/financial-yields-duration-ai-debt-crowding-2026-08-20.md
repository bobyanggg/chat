# Research Report — Yields, Duration, and AI Debt Crowding (through 2026-08-20)

> **Type:** financial (event axis) | **Date:** 2026-08-20 | **Constraints:** US; late July–20 August 2026
>
> **Assumptions:** Official CMT (Treasury par yield curve / FRED H.15) is the close series; on-the-run/intraday quotes are labeled separately. "$1.5 trillion" is treated as a lease-commitment stock unless a reputable source uses it as 2026 issuance.

## Yields, Duration, and AI Debt Crowding

Official Constant Maturity Treasury (CMT) par yields, published by the U.S. Treasury from New York Fed bid-side quotes near 3:30 p.m., show a long-end backup that was already underway before the 18 August equity session and that peaked on an official-close basis on 17 August, not on 18 August. According to Treasury’s July and August daily par yield curve tables, the 10-year CMT closed at 4.61% and the 30-year at 5.09% on 28 July 2026, then jumped 6 basis points and 11 basis points respectively on 29 July to 4.67% and 5.20%, and finished the month at 4.75% and 5.27% on 31 July. After a partial retracement in the first half of August (10-year 4.63% and 30-year 5.17% on 5 August; 4.63% and 5.21% on 13 August), a second leg carried the 30-year CMT to 5.31% on 17 August and the 10-year to 4.72% that day—the highest official 30-year close in the late-July-to-19-August window. On 18 August the CMT 10-year eased one basis point to 4.71% and the 30-year three basis points to 5.28%. The 19 August buyback announcement took official closes to 4.65% and 5.19%. Those CMT prints match the Federal Reserve H.15 series republished on FRED as DGS10 and DGS30 through 18 August (4.71% and 5.28%). Treasury and FRED are Primary sources. [U.S. Treasury daily par yield curve, August 2026](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?field_tdr_date_value_month=202608&type=daily_treasury_yield_curve) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [U.S. Treasury daily par yield curve, July 2026](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?field_tdr_date_value_month=202607&type=daily_treasury_yield_curve) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [FRED DGS10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS10) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [FRED DGS30](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS30) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High).

The much-cited ~5.34% 30-year print on 18 August is an on-the-run/intraday market quote, not the official CMT close. Reuters, reprinted by Channel NewsAsia, put the 30-year at 5.3371% at the session high on 18 August and 5.2922% later, with the 10-year as high as 4.7478% before 4.712%. CNN Business wrote that the 30-year “hit its highest level since 2007, rising to 5.34% before edging down slightly,” and that the 10-year hit 4.74%. CNBC’s morning wrap had the 30-year at 5.335% at 7:38 a.m. ET. Trading Economics later described a 19-year high of 5.34% and, on 20 August, showed the buyback relief reversing, with the 10-year near 4.71% and the 30-year near 5.24%. This pattern indicates that 18 August was the on-the-run peak; 17 August was the CMT close peak; and 19 August was the official-yield reversal. [Channel NewsAsia / Reuters](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/business/stocks-slide-yields-elevated-mideast-conflict-fears-grow-6326331) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [CNN Business](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/18/investing/global-bond-market) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [CNBC, 18 Aug](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/18/bond-yields-government-us10y-treasury-note-iran.html) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/united-states/government-bond-yield) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: Medium).

Dating when the backup “started” depends on the window. Relative to late July, the clearest discrete jump is 29 July, after the 28 July local low. Relative to a slightly longer horizon, the long end had already been rising from 30 June, when Treasury CMT 10-year and 30-year closes were 4.44% and 4.91%, so the 17 August 30-year CMT of 5.31% is a 40-basis-point rise from month-end June and a 22-basis-point rise from the 28 July trough. CNBC’s 19 August buyback story described a “buyers’ strike” in the 10- to 30-year sector “since late June.” That longer-horizon description is consistent with the June 30 starting point, but it is not the same as a late-July start. [U.S. Treasury, June 2026](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?field_tdr_date_value_month=202606&type=daily_treasury_yield_curve) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [CNBC, 19 Aug buyback](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/19/treasury-announces-upscaled-buyback-operation-for-longer-term-debt-sending-yields-lower.html) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: Medium).

Established desks on 18 August treated higher long-end yields as the mechanism that hit technology, even when they traced the yield move itself to oil and the Iran stalemate. Reuters’ close wrap, reprinted by RTHK and The Business Times, reported that the Nasdaq Composite fell 1.33% to 26,289.71, the S&P 500 0.69% to 7,691.76, the Dow 0.22%, S&P information technology 1.9%, and the Philadelphia SE Semiconductor Index 5.0%, “marking the biggest daily percentage decline since July 29” for the S&P and Nasdaq, with Nvidia down 2.3% and Micron 7%. Burns McKinney, portfolio manager at NFJ Investment Group, described a “domino effect”: “Talks break down. That leads to oil prices going up. That leads to higher inflation expectations and bond yields rise,” and added that “every time bond yields rise, that tends to disproportionately hit the technology names.” Tony Welch, CIO at SignatureFD, said “There’s nothing that can crack a momentum rally quite like interest rates moving higher and you’re getting evidence of that today,” and argued that the yield rise suggested Fed policy was too easy for the growth and inflation outlook. Reuters’ own copy stated that rising borrowing costs “lowered how much investors were willing to pay for potential growth in technology profits,” and a same-day Reuters futures piece said high government yields “potentially lower the present value of future tech profits and increase corporate borrowing costs.” Those are Established-tier desk and wire formulations of the duration and discount-rate channel, not independent academic estimates of present-value sensitivity. [The Business Times / Reuters](https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/companies-markets/capital-markets-currencies/us-stocks-tech-selloff-weighs-down-wall-street-bond-yields-climb) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [RTHK / Reuters](https://news.rthk.hk/rthk/en/component/k2/1866648-20260819.htm?spTabChangeable=0) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [Reuters futures wrap](https://www.reuters.com/business/us-stock-futures-drop-fading-iran-peace-hopes-lift-oil-bond-yields-2026-08-18/) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: Medium).

Kim Forrest, CIO at Bokeh Capital Partners, tied the same yield backup to AI-specific borrowing and payback risk. In a Reuters interview reprinted by The Journal Record, she said: “The yields are troubling people because it portends a tighter environment and it’s going to be more expensive to borrow money. Especially in this whole AI thing where time to pay it back is uncertain. It makes for a nervous investor environment.” That is a single-desk quote (Medium confidence as attribution; the quote is corroborated across Reuters reprints). It is analysis, not a measured elasticity of AI IRRs to the 30-year. [The Journal Record / Reuters](https://journalrecord.com/?p=765994) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: Medium).

Carl Weinberg of High Frequency Economics, on CNBC’s Squawk Box Europe on 18 August, supplied the crowding-out formulation. He said AI infrastructure, technology and utilities had “borrowed a lot of money,” citing “estimates of up to $600 billion borrowed over the last year, with a further $200 billion worth of funding, borrowing, new issuance and IPOs coming in the hopper already.” He argued that this draws on “the same pool of savings” that funds the government deficit, creating a second “hyper borrower” in the “collective AI enterprise,” so that “they and the government together are crowding out investment by small businesses, and that’s what’s driving up bond yields,” including abroad via capital flowing to the United States. CNBC’s video slug the same morning framed the argument as crowding out rather than bond vigilantes, rate-hike odds, or inflation. Weinberg’s $600 billion figure is an unnamed estimate he had “seen,” not a named vendor series; treat it as a single-source directional claim, not a 2026 issuance total. [CNBC, 18 Aug](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/18/bond-yields-government-us10y-treasury-note-iran.html) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: Medium); [CNBC video, 18 Aug](https://www.cnbc.com/video/2026/08/18/whos-behind-the-bond-bear-market-hfes-weinberg-explains.html) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: Medium).

Krishna Guha of Evercore ISI used the “tidal wave of hyperscaler debt” language on 19 August, after the buyback, not as an 18 August equity-wrap quote. In a client note cited by CNBC, PBS/AP, and The Fiscal Times, Guha said stepped-up buybacks could crowd in buyers and force short-covering, “But the operation changes almost nothing in terms of the fundamentals in particular the unchanged need to finance the tidal wave of hyperscaler debt in addition to very large government deficits.” AP, via PBS NewsHour, glossed hyperscalers as Big Tech firms “borrowing mountains of money to build AI data centers,” with those bonds “competing with U.S. Treasurys for buyers, which can push bond yields higher.” CNN’s 18 August bond feature made the same crowding point without Guha, quoting deVere Group CEO Nigel Green: “Hyperscaler borrowing to fund AI infrastructure is competing for the same pool of buyers at the same moment governments need those buyers most.” [CNBC, 19 Aug](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/19/treasury-announces-upscaled-buyback-operation-for-longer-term-debt-sending-yields-lower.html) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [PBS NewsHour / AP](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/economy/an-alarmed-bond-market-gets-the-trump-administration-to-act-again) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [CNN Business](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/18/investing/global-bond-market) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: Medium).

Reputable issuance figures for 2026 exist, but they are not a single number and they are not the unconfirmed “$1.5 trillion” blog figure sometimes attached to this tape. Reuters’ 29 July analysis of LSEG data, reprinted by Channel NewsAsia, found that Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Oracle issued about $194 billion of bonds in 2026 through 7 July, up 79% from roughly $108 billion in all of 2025; Goldman Sachs, cited in the same piece, expected issuance by five hyperscalers including Microsoft of roughly $250 billion in 2026 and $400 billion in 2027, against about $750 billion of hyperscaler capex in 2026. Sage Advisory, also in that Reuters story, put the hyperscalers’ combined U.S.-dollar debt footprint at more than $360 billion, more than double since September. PIMCO, using Bloomberg index data as of 4 August, reported a different metric: 2026 year-to-date net supply of index-eligible AI hyperscaler debt of $120 billion in USD IG and €22 billion in EUR IG, with hyperscalers nearly 5% of the USD IG index. Reuters Breakingviews (Jamie McGeever), reprinted 19–20 August by The Globe and Mail, cited SIFMA for $1.68 trillion of all U.S. corporate issuance from January through mid-August, up nearly 27% year over year, of which SIFMA’s “high technology” sector was 12.8% (financials 45.2%), and LSEG for AI/Big Tech issuance already above $220 billion in 2026, double last year’s total. Morgan Stanley, via a 3 June Reuters story, had earlier put global debt raised by “Meta, Oracle and other technology companies” at $250 billion “this year,” and Hindu Business Line later cited Morgan Stanley at nearly $236 billion of global AI-related debt by 31 May with a $570 billion full-year 2026 forecast. Those Morgan Stanley numbers are a broader global-AI universe and an earlier cutoff; they should not be added to the Reuters/LSEG four-name $194 billion print. [Channel NewsAsia / Reuters, 29 Jul](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/business/hyperscaler-debt-binge-pushes-yields-up-investor-demand-cools-6286196) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [PIMCO Credit Market Lens, 10 Aug](https://www.pimco.com/us/en/insights/the-credit-market-lens-the-ai-split-between-us-dollar-and-euro-investment-grade) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [The Globe and Mail / Reuters Breakingviews](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/investing/article-dont-blame-us-bond-market-indigestion-on-ai-debt-binge/) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High).

The $1.5 trillion figure that circulates in some market commentary is, in established reporting, a lease-commitment stock, not 2026 bond issuance. Goldman Sachs analysts, cited by CNBC on 14 August, estimated hyperscalers’ combined lease commitments for data centers, R&D, offices and equipment at $1.5 trillion, up from about $200 billion five years earlier, including about $1 trillion of “uncommenced” leases not yet on financial statements. A 4 August Reuters compilation of filings put uncommenced lease payments for Microsoft, Meta, Oracle, Amazon and Alphabet at about $1.09 trillion, later nearer $1.16 trillion after Meta’s July leases. Those are Primary/Established measures of off-balance-sheet capacity commitments. They corroborate that AI funding is larger than cash-bond issuance, but they are not a 2026 supply print and should not be used as one. [CNBC, 14 Aug](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/14/ai-infrastructure-debt-leverage-risks.html) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High).

Whether hyperscaler duration actually moved Treasury yields, as opposed to coinciding with them, is contested among established sources. Guha, Weinberg, CNN’s Green, and a Dallas Fed working paper cited by McGeever treat extra corporate duration as a term-premium/crowding channel. McGeever’s Breakingviews column, however, answers its own crowding question “not necessarily,” noting that the sovereign backup was global and that Goldman Sachs strategists put AI-related financing at almost a quarter of gross IG issuance yet estimated only about five basis points of spillover to broader U.S. rates from AI issuance over the last year, writing that “AI’s contribution to U.S. GDP growth and its crowding out effects are smaller than often thought.” Goldman’s tight non-AI credit spreads are the main evidence offered for limited spillover. The 18 August tech tape does not resolve that debate: equity desks priced duration and borrowing-cost pain in growth names whether the 30-year was being driven by Iran/oil, deficits, Warsh communication, Japanese yields, or AI supply. This suggests the equity channel (discount rates and AI funding costs) can operate even if the Treasury crowding channel is only a few basis points. [The Globe and Mail / Reuters Breakingviews](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/investing/article-dont-blame-us-bond-market-indigestion-on-ai-debt-binge/) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: Medium).

On 18 August wraps, long-end yields remained the lead explanation for the technology selloff, usually as the middle link in an Iran–oil–inflation–yields–duration chain rather than as a standalone “AI debt crowding” headline. Reuters’ equity close led with semiconductors and “bond yields to multiyear peaks, feeding concerns about borrowing costs and inflation,” and quoted McKinney and Welch on yields hitting tech. Channel NewsAsia’s Reuters markets wrap said elevated yields weigh on equities “by making stocks relatively less attractive to investors, and by raising borrowing costs for companies pouring money into capital-intensive AI infrastructure.” CNN’s same-day feature was a global-bond story that listed deficits, Iran/oil, Warsh communication, and hyperscaler corporate supply, then noted that the Nasdaq fell 1.3% as a consequence of yields. AI-debt crowding was therefore present in 18 August bond coverage and in Weinberg’s CNBC hit; it was not the lead line of the equity close wrap. [The Business Times / Reuters](https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/companies-markets/capital-markets-currencies/us-stocks-tech-selloff-weighs-down-wall-street-bond-yields-climb) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [Channel NewsAsia / Reuters](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/business/stocks-slide-yields-elevated-mideast-conflict-fears-grow-6326331) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High); [CNN Business](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/18/investing/global-bond-market) (accessed 2026-08-20, confidence: High).

The 19 August Treasury buyback reversed the yield tape and lifted some major indexes modestly; it did not reverse the technology tape. Treasury’s own statement said it would at least double liquidity-support buybacks in the 10- to 20-year and 20- to 30-year nominal sectors from a $2 billion maximum to at least $4 billion per operation, effective 9 September through 4 November 2026, framed as liquidity support rather than a debt paydown. Official 30-year CMT fell 12 basis points from the 17 August high close of 5.31% to 5.19% on 19 August; CNBC’s cash-market quotes had the 30-year down more than 10 basis points to 5.184% and the 10-year more than 6 basis points to 4.637% at one point. Primary index closes, matching CNBC, put the S&P 500 up 0.21% to 7,707.98 and the Nasdaq Composite up 0.16% to 26,331.09, after much larger morning gains (CNBC: Nasdaq as much as +0.6%, S&P and Dow about +0.7%) faded. That Composite close recovered only a small fraction of Tuesday’s 355-point, 1.33% drop. The Nasdaq-100 did not join even that modest bounce: Yahoo daily bars close NDX at 29,490.96 on 18 August (−1.68%) and 29,426.02 on 19 August (−0.22%). The Philadelphia SE Semiconductor Index, which had fallen 4.98% on 18 August, fell another 2.12% to 11,738.23 on 19 August. CNBC also reported Broadcom down more than 4%, AMD nearly 4%, and the iShares AI Innovation and Tech Active ETF down about 2% after a Wall Street Journal report that OpenAI’s second-quarter results disappointed. Reuters’ 19 August VIEW note had the Nasdaq up 0.4% while yields were still falling, which is consistent with CNBC’s description of a rally that faded into the close. Low-tier recaps that print Nasdaq +0.59% to 26,445.73 as a close appear to have captured the intraday high, not the official close. On 20 August, established wires said the yield relief was fading: Reuters had the 30-year up about 3 basis points to 5.2235% after a 5.1765% low; Bloomberg, via SWI swissinfo, had the 10-year at 4.69% and the 30-year at 5.23% near midday with the Nasdaq 100 down 0.5%; Trading Economics had the 10-year near 4.71% and the 30-year near 5.24%, erasing the buyback drop. Official CMT for 20 August had not yet appeared on Treasury’s table at the time of this research. Fact versus synthesis: the CMT path, the 18 August index declines, the named desk quotes, the Reuters/LSEG $194 billion four-name print, Goldman’s $250 billion five-name 2026 forecast, PIMCO’s $120 billion net USD figure, SIFMA’s $1.68 trillion all-corporate total, and the 19 August index closes versus lingering chip losses are sourced facts. That high yields hit tech because distant cash flows are discounted harder, and that AI capex financed with debt makes the sector more rate-sensitive, is the established desk consensus on 18 August. That hyperscaler supply was the dominant driver of the 30-year backup, as opposed to oil/Iran, deficits, or Fed-chair communication, is not a settled fact; Goldman’s ~5 basis-point crowding estimate and the global character of the bond move are the main established counterweights.
